Chapter 1 · Part I · The Diagnosis

You don’t just have a time problem. You have an attention problem.

On how you use your time, on what you give your attention to, and on the call to live wisely before God.

1. Time is not always the problem

Have you ever said, “I don’t have time to read the Bible,” and then spent an hour or two on your phone without noticing how the time went by?

Maybe you meant to get up earlier to pray, but you hit snooze. Maybe you wanted to study a chapter of Scripture, but you ended up watching videos that gave you nothing of value. Maybe you ended the day regretting that you could have done more, even though you had the same twenty-four hours as the day before.

If you recognize yourself in these situations, you need to make an important distinction: sometimes you really do have too many responsibilities, you are tired, or you need rest. At other times, the problem is not a lack of time but the way you use it.

And sometimes the problem is more subtle. You have the time, but you can no longer keep your attention on one thing long enough to engage with it for real.

You open the Bible, but you are thinking about notifications. You start to pray, but your mind drifts to the conversations on your phone. You try to read a few pages, but you feel the need to check whether anything new has happened.

You end up physically present before the Word of God, with your attention divided in ten directions.

This is where one of the important struggles of contemporary Christian life begins: the struggle to manage your time and to keep your attention fixed on the things that matter.

You cannot control every circumstance of your life. You cannot add hours to a day. But you can learn to make wiser decisions about what you do with the time you have.

It is not enough to want a deeper spiritual life. You have to create the conditions in which that desire can become practice.

2. “Redeeming the time”: what does Scripture actually say?

One of the foundational passages of this book is found in Ephesians 5:15–17.

In this context, the apostle Paul urges believers to walk carefully, not as unwise people but as wise, to redeem the time, and to understand what the will of the Lord is.

Notice the order of the ideas.

Paul is not talking about time management in a modern productivity manual. He is talking about how people who follow Christ are to live. The exhortation is part of a larger section about the believer’s walk, light, wisdom, and discernment.

So we should not pull the phrase “redeeming the time” out of its context and turn it into a slogan about efficiency.

What does “redeeming the time” mean?

The Greek expression used in Ephesians 5:16 is exagorazomenoi ton kairon (ἐξαγοραζόμενοι τὸν καιρόν).

  • Exagorazō means, in its basic sense, to buy or to buy back.
  • Kairos refers to a time, a moment, or an opportunity, depending on the context.

In this passage, the idea is to use wisely the opportunities you have, in a world where evil is a reality. It does not mean that we can literally buy time, nor that every minute must be filled with religious activity.

Notice verse 17 as well: Paul goes on to urge believers to understand what the will of the Lord is.

So the question is not only:

“How can I get more done in a day?”

The deeper question is:

“How can I use the time I have in a way that reflects the wisdom and will of God?”

This shift in perspective matters.

You can have an extremely busy day, check off dozens of tasks, and still neglect the essential things. Productivity is not automatically the same as wisdom. Neither does a schedule packed with Christian activities prove spiritual maturity by itself.

The goal is not to become a machine that carries out religious tasks. The goal is to learn to live with discernment before God.

3. Your attention shows, in practice, what gets priority

In Matthew 6:21, Jesus says that where a person’s treasure is, there his heart will be also.

In context, He is speaking about treasures, attachments, and the relationship between earthly possessions and priorities before God. He is not speaking about smartphones, of course. It would be anachronistic to claim otherwise.

Still, the principle invites serious self-examination.

The things we value tend to receive our time, our energy, and our attention. Not always perfectly and not without exceptions, but repeated choices often reveal what holds an important place in our lives.

You may say the Bible is important to you. You may say prayer is a priority. You may confess that you want to follow Christ.

But it is healthy to ask whether the way you organize your days supports these convictions.

Not to condemn yourself, but to see reality.

If you repeatedly spend a lot of time on activities you can’t even remember at the end of the day, yet never find a slot for prayer, there is a contradiction between your intentions and the system you live by.

This contradiction does not automatically mean you don’t love God. It means you need to examine what is happening and change what is within your power to change.

Maybe you need more rest. Maybe you have formed a habit of checking your phone whenever a moment of boredom appears. Maybe you have waited too long for motivation to show up, instead of building a routine.

Or maybe you set a goal so ambitious that, instead of starting, you keep putting it off.

You will not solve these things by simply telling yourself you have to be more disciplined. You need to identify the concrete mechanism that is holding you back.

An essential distinction

There is a difference between feeling that you are not good enough and recognizing that you have a behavior that needs to change.

The first state can keep you stuck in guilt. The second gives you a starting point.

In Romans 8:1, Paul states that there is no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. This truth does not cancel responsibility or the call to holiness. It reminds us that our relationship with God must not be reduced to an endless attempt to earn acceptance through performance.

You can acknowledge your lack of discipline without concluding that there is no hope for you.

You can repent of neglect, ask for God’s help, and begin to act differently.

Grace is not an excuse to stay in the same habits. But neither is discipline a method of buying God’s love.

4. The phone is not the problem in itself. Your relationship with it can become the problem.

The phone is a tool. You can use it to read Scripture, listen to sermons, communicate with family, learn, and carry out your responsibilities.

There is no point in turning technology into an absolute enemy.

The problem appears when using it becomes hard to control, conflicts with your responsibilities, or repeatedly takes the time you meant to use differently.

Think about the following situation.

You have ten free minutes. You open your phone to check a message. After you reply, you notice a notification. Then you see a recommended video. After that one, another appears. You did not consciously decide to spend the next forty minutes this way. You moved from one action to the next without checking your original intention.

Here it helps to understand the difference between intention and automatic behavior.

An intention is what you decided to do. An automatic behavior is an action that happens with little deliberation, often because you have repeated it in the same contexts.

For example:

  • You are bored and you open your phone.
  • You face a difficult task and you check your messages.
  • You feel tired and you start scrolling through videos.
  • You wake up in the morning and check your notifications before you get out of bed.

Over time, these associations can become stronger. You don’t need to assume you have a clinical problem to recognize a habit that no longer serves your goals.

Don’t rely on willpower alone

If the phone is next to you, the screen lights up, and notifications interrupt you, you have to make the decision not to check it again and again.

If, on the other hand, you leave it in another room during Bible study and turn off non-essential notifications, you reduce the number of times you have to resist temptation.

This is a simple but important idea: discipline does not only mean resisting a habit; it also means arranging your environment so that the good choice is easier to make.

It is not a lack of faith to use phone settings, app limits, or a separate alarm clock. They are practical tools for managing your attention better.

In 1 Corinthians 6:12, Paul discusses freedom and mastery. His principle is relevant: not everything that is permitted is helpful, and we must not come under the power of anything.

The context of the passage must be respected, but the principle invites us to examine our freedom seriously.

Ask yourself: am I using this tool deliberately, or do I end up doing what it dictates through notifications, recommendations, and impulses?

You don’t have to throw your phone away. You have to learn to use it without letting it organize your whole day.

5. Why you can’t build a deep spiritual life on impulses alone

Maybe you have attended a Christian gathering that motivated you. You heard a powerful sermon, you were searched by a biblical truth, or you felt an intense desire for change.

In that moment, you told yourself that starting tomorrow you would read the Bible for two hours, pray more, and give up the habits that consume your time.

But after a few days, the enthusiasm faded.

What do you do then?

If your whole plan rested on the intensity of an emotion, you may quit once the emotion is gone.

Here we need to distinguish between motivation and discipline.

Motivation can help you start. Discipline helps you keep going when the desire is no longer as intense. And wisdom helps you choose a pace you can sustain without neglecting your health, your rest, and your responsibilities.

In Luke 16:10, Jesus speaks about faithfulness in little things and in much. The context of the passage is the responsible use of what we have been entrusted with. We should not turn the verse into a promise that any small routine will automatically produce extraordinary results.

Still, it gives us a relevant principle for forming character: faithfulness is expressed not only in spectacular moments but also in how we manage ordinary responsibilities.

Applied to your spiritual life, this may mean keeping a daily slot for Scripture even on an ordinary day, without waiting for the perfect atmosphere to appear.

Perhaps it means praying for ten minutes when you are tired, instead of abandoning prayer altogether because you can’t sustain an hour.

Perhaps it means carefully reading and understanding one passage, instead of rushing through five chapters just to check off a goal.

Don’t belittle small beginnings. But don’t use the idea of small steps as an excuse to stop growing, either.

If you want to reach two or three hours of Bible study and prayer a day, you can work in that direction. But build up progressively, with a realistic schedule, and check whether that time produces understanding, sincere prayer, and obedience, not just the ticking off of numbers.

A deep spiritual life is not measured only in hours, but neither is it built without time, attention, and consistency.

6. Attention is also a form of responsible stewardship

In Psalm 90:12, the psalmist asks: “So teach us to count our days, that we may gain a heart of wisdom.”

This prayer reminds us that our time is limited and that we need wisdom to use it.

Counting our days does not mean living constantly under the pressure of a stopwatch. It means recognizing that life is not unlimited and that our choices matter.

There is time for work, study, prayer, relationships, rest, and service. We do not have to turn all of these into a competition in which religious activities automatically win every scheduling conflict. We have to learn to arrange them wisely, keeping God at the center of our lives.

In the Gospels, we see Jesus withdrawing to lonely places to pray, for example in Luke 5:16. This detail is part of the account of His ministry and shows us that prayer held a real place in His life.

We cannot deduce from this verse a universal prayer schedule for every Christian. But we can observe the importance of withdrawing from the commotion to pray.

Sometimes, to pray with attention, you have to interrupt whatever is demanding your attention.

You have to close the app. Put the phone aside. Choose a place and a time. Accept that you will not respond instantly to every notification.

Not because all notifications are bad, but because not all of them deserve to interrupt what you have decided to do.

Learn to be present.

When you read, read. When you pray, pray. When you work, work. When you rest, rest.

This is not an invitation to rigidity. It is an invitation to a life that is less fragmented and better directed.

7. Practical exercise: the attention audit

You cannot realistically change a behavior you refuse to examine. That is why, before you build your new schedule, you need an honest picture of how you use your time.

For the next three days, don’t try to radically transform your routine. Observe your behavior.

Day 1: measure

Check your phone’s screen-time feature.

Write down:

  • How much time you spent on your phone.
  • Which apps you used the most.
  • Which use was necessary.
  • Which use was intentional.
  • Which use went on without a clear purpose.

Don’t automatically treat every minute spent on your phone as wasted time. A call with your family, a study session, and an hour of aimless scrolling are different things.

Day 2: identify the triggers

Every time you notice that you have opened your phone without a clear reason, note the context.

Were you bored? Tired? Restless? Avoiding a difficult task? Waiting for a message?

The goal is not to judge yourself for every impulse, but to discover the patterns that repeat.

Day 3: change just one thing

Choose one concrete change:

  • Don’t use your phone for the first 20–30 minutes after you wake up.
  • Leave it in another room during your first Bible study session.
  • Turn off the notifications you don’t need.
  • Set clear time slots for social media.
  • Replace a period of aimless scrolling with reading a Bible passage and a few minutes of prayer.

Choose a single change you can maintain. Once it becomes more stable, add another.

You don’t need to prove in a single day that you can live perfectly. You need to start living more consciously.

8. Spiritual exercise: examine your priorities before God

In your next prayer session, don’t begin by immediately asking for more discipline. Take a few minutes to examine your life honestly.

You can use the following questions:

  • What activities take up most of my time each day?
  • What do I do when I have a few free minutes?
  • What am I avoiding when I open my phone?
  • What place does Scripture hold in my real schedule, not just in my intentions?
  • Do I want to know God more deeply, or am I mostly seeking the feeling that I have done enough?
  • What concrete change can I make today to manage my time better?
  • Is there a responsibility, a relationship, or a need for rest that I am neglecting in my attempt to improve my routine?

The last question is important. Christian discipline does not mean ignoring all your other responsibilities in the name of a list of spiritual goals. It means learning to live wisely, in obedience to God and with responsibility toward the people around you.

Prayer

Lord, help me look honestly at how I use my time. Show me the habits that distract me from my responsibilities, and help me change what is within my power to change.

Give me wisdom to organize my days, patience to build healthy habits, and consistency in prayer and in reading Scripture. Keep me from the pride that makes me compare myself to others and from the discouragement that makes me quit when I fail.

Help me not to pursue merely a more efficient schedule, but to grow in knowing You, in love, in obedience, and in serving my neighbor. Teach me to use wisely the time You have entrusted to me.

Amen.

9. Reflection: what should you take with you from this chapter?

Before you move on to the next chapter, stop and answer the following questions in writing.

About reality

  • What is the biggest needless consumer of time in my life?
  • When am I most vulnerable to distractions?
  • What important responsibility have I repeatedly put off?

About faith

  • What does it mean for me to redeem the time in light of the passage in Ephesians 5:15–17?
  • How can I organize my spiritual life without turning my relationship with God into a performance system?
  • What biblical truth do I need to apply, not just know?

About action

  • What change will I make in the next 24 hours?
  • When will I have my first session of prayer and Bible study?
  • How will I evaluate at the end of the week whether I have made progress?

Don’t answer with what you think would sound good in a Christian book. Answer with what is true about your life.

If you need to change ten things, start by identifying the first step. If you have tried many times and given up, analyze why that happened. If you are already consistent, look for ways to go deeper into what you are doing.

There is no reason to compare yourself with anyone else’s pace. You need to be honest before God and act with wisdom.

Conclusion: don’t let intention replace obedience

You can spend years telling yourself that someday you will become more disciplined. You can buy books on productivity, listen to sermons on prayer, and collect plans you never put into practice.

But knowing a truth is not the same as living it.

In Matthew 7:24–27, Jesus closes the Sermon on the Mount with the picture of the two houses. He compares the person who hears His words and does them with the person who hears them but does not do them. The decisive difference is not the mere fact that both heard, but their response to what they heard.

This is a fitting starting point for our whole journey.

You should not finish this book with the feeling that you have collected one more set of interesting ideas. You should begin to examine how you live and to apply what you understand from Scripture.

You will not have the same energy every day. You will not always manage to follow the plan you set. Sometimes you will have to adjust your schedule, rest, or return after a period of neglect.

But don’t confuse a slip with abandonment, and don’t let regret become a substitute for change.

Today you don’t have to fix your whole life. You have to begin by seeing the truth, making a wise decision, and taking the first step.

Redeem the time. Not to prove that you are a better Christian than others, but to live with more wisdom, faithfulness, and availability before God.

Chapter 2 · Part I · The Diagnosis

The Phone: Tool or Master?

“All things are lawful for me, but not all things are expedient. All things are lawful for me, but I will not be brought under the power of any.” — 1 Corinthians 6:12

1. The problem is not that you have a phone, but that the phone can end up having you

The phone is one of the most useful tools people have ever made. With it you can communicate with your family, learn, read the Bible, listen to sermons, work, and spread the truth of the Gospel.

The same device can consume hours of your time without giving you anything of real value. You can start the day by reading a verse and, a few minutes later, find yourself watching dozens of videos that have nothing to do with what you were looking for in the first place.

You can open your phone to check a message and close it forty minutes later, without remembering very well what you did.

You can say you have no time for prayer, yet discover that you had time for plenty of things that were neither urgent nor even important.

Here we have to be honest: sometimes the problem is not a lack of time but the way we use it.

Still, not every long stretch of phone use automatically means a lack of discipline. Maybe you are studying, working, talking with someone who needs help, or carrying out your responsibilities. It is not the number of minutes that tells the whole story, but the purpose, the effect, and the control you have over the habit.

The question is not only: “How much time do I spend on my phone?”

The more important questions are:

  • Why do I open it? Do I have a clear purpose, or am I automatically looking for something to occupy my mind?
  • What happens to me after I use it? Am I better informed and better prepared, or just more agitated and more distracted?
  • Can I stop? Can I put it aside when I have decided to pray, read, study, or carry out a responsibility?

These questions move the discussion from the mere use of technology to self-control.

2. The biblical principle of freedom: not everything that is permitted is good for you

In 1 Corinthians 6:12, Paul states two important principles: not everything that is permitted is helpful, and we must not come under the power of anything.

The immediate context of the passage concerns moral behavior and Christian freedom. It would not be right to claim that Paul is talking directly about smartphones, apps, or social media. They did not exist in their present form.

Still, his principle is relevant to how we use technology.

Something can be permitted without being helpful in every situation. An activity can be neutral in itself yet become harmful through excess, through the moment we choose it, or through the effect it has on our responsibilities.

Let’s take a few examples:

  • It is permitted to relax, but relaxation can become a way of constantly running from responsibilities.
  • It is permitted to follow Christian content, but you can end up consuming sermons without applying what you learn.
  • It is permitted to use social media, but you can end up constantly comparing your life with the lives presented by others.
  • It is permitted to be entertained, but entertainment can take the place of the things you promised you would do.

Notice something: even seemingly good activities can be used in an unbalanced way.

You can listen to ten sermons about prayer and not pray. You can save hundreds of verses and not meditate on a single one. You can share messages about discipline and put off, this very day, the thing you know you need to do.

Knowing the truth is not the same as living it.

James 1:22 urges us to be doers of the Word, not hearers only. In context, the warning concerns our response to the Word of God, not time spent on a screen. But the application is clear: spiritual information must lead to obedience, not just to accumulation.

You don’t just need more Christian content. You need to respond faithfully to the truth you already know.

3. How does a habit gain power over you?

We rarely lose control of a habit in a single day. Most of the time, things change gradually.

At first, you open an app for a few minutes. Then you notice that you open it whenever you are bored. Later, you end up opening it without thinking, even when you have something else to do.

A repeating loop can form:

1. A trigger

You are bored, tired, restless, or you want a break.

2. Automatic reaction

You pick up your phone and open the usual app.

3. The immediate reward

You get novelty, entertainment, or a temporary distraction.

4. The repetition

The next time the same trigger appears, the reaction becomes easier to repeat.

This is a simplified model of habit formation, not a complete explanation of all behaviors. But it helps us identify something important: if you want to change a habit, it is not enough to tell yourself you need more willpower. You also have to change the conditions in which the habit repeats.

If the phone stays next to you all the time, notifications keep coming, and the apps that distract you are one tap away, you are making discipline harder than it needs to be.

You don’t have to turn every day into a heroic battle against a screen. You can arrange your environment so that the good choice is easier to make.

For example:

  • If you grab your phone right after waking up, keep it out of arm’s reach at night.
  • If notifications interrupt you during prayer, turn on Do Not Disturb.
  • If you automatically go to social media, remove the apps from your home screen or use time limits.
  • If you can’t concentrate when the phone is next to your Bible, put it in another room.
  • If you use it for study, prepare the materials you need in advance, so you don’t end up browsing aimlessly.

These measures are not signs of weakness. They are forms of practical wisdom.

In Matthew 26:41, Jesus tells the disciples to watch and pray, acknowledging the weakness of human nature. The context is the prayer in Gethsemane and the approach of His arrest. It is not a passage about technology, but it reminds us that vigilance must be accompanied by an acknowledgment of our own limits.

Don’t rely only on your intentions. Build an environment that helps you carry them out.

4. Your attention is a limited resource

In a single day you can receive hundreds of pieces of information, messages, and demands. But you can’t give them all the same attention, and your mind needs periods in which to focus on one thing.

That is why it matters not only what you consume, but also how you consume it.

You can read a chapter of the Bible while checking your messages, changing the song, and responding to notifications. You have spent time with the text, but you may have understood less than you would have by reading it without interruptions.

You can listen to a sermon while browsing other apps. The sound reaches you, but that doesn’t mean you have reflected on the message.

You can sit next to someone and be so absorbed in your phone that you are not really present.

The problem is not that every distraction does deep harm. The problem is that repeated distractions can make it harder to sustain your attention when an activity requires patience.

And important things often require patience.

In Psalm 1, the blessed person is described as meditating day and night on the Law of the Lord. The picture is not of someone who merely encounters information, but of someone who keeps returning to the Word and lets it shape their thinking.

Biblical meditation does not mean reading as fast as possible, but pausing, understanding, and responding.

When you read a passage, ask yourself:

  • What does the text actually say?
  • What do I learn about God, about humanity, and about the relationship between them?
  • What do I need to believe, correct, or put into practice?
  • Is there something I need to do today in response to what I have read?

You don’t have to receive a spectacular revelation from every reading. Sometimes the most important response is simple obedience, done without applause and without anyone seeing it.

5. Don’t confuse religious activity with closeness to God

There is a trap more subtle than wasting time on entertainment: using even religious activities to avoid a personal encounter with the truth.

You can watch sermons for hours but never stop to pray. You can look up complex biblical explanations but ignore a simple command you have already understood. You can talk about spiritual discipline but never examine your own habits.

Not all consumption of Christian content is superficial. Sermons, courses, and books can be valuable resources. The question is whether they help you grow in the knowledge of God and in obedience, or whether they have become a substitute for practicing your faith.

In Luke 10:38–42, Martha is busy serving, while Mary sits at Jesus’ feet and listens to Him. Jesus does not condemn every practical responsibility, nor does He say that work doesn’t matter. He points out that Martha is anxious and troubled about many things, while Mary has chosen what was essential at that moment.

This passage invites us to examine not only the quantity of our activities but also the place we give to Christ in the midst of them.

You can be busy with good things and still lose sight of what is essential.

That is why you should not measure spiritual growth solely by the number of sermons heard, chapters read, or hours spent in religious activities. Also ask yourself whether you are becoming more faithful, more attentive to the truth, more ready to forgive, more responsible, and more willing to obey God.

You don’t do these things to earn God’s love. Christian obedience is a response to His grace, not a method of forcing Him to accept you.

6. Discipline does not mean hating your phone

There is a risk of swinging from one extreme to the other. You notice that your phone is eating your time, and you decide you must give up every form of entertainment, eliminate every pause, and fill every minute with spiritual activity.

But God has not asked you to prove you are faithful through exhaustion.

Rest is important. Relationships are important. Work, study, family responsibilities, and healthy moments of relaxation have their place. Even Jesus called the disciples to withdraw and rest a while, in Mark 6:31.

The context speaks of the disciples returning from ministry and their need for rest. So we cannot equate rest with laziness, nor should we feel guilty for every moment in which we aren’t producing something.

The goal is not to become a person who never relaxes. The goal is not to use relaxation to avoid what matters, again and again.

Likewise, the goal is not to spend every free minute reading the Bible. The goal is to give God a real and consistent place in your life and to let His Word influence your choices.

A disciplined Christian is not someone who never departs from the plan. It is someone who learns to return to what is important, to admit mistakes, and to continue without turning every failure into a condemnation of themselves.

We are not seeking absolute control over every minute, but a life managed wisely before God.

7. Practical exercise: regain control for seven days

Don’t try to change all your habits in a single day. Choose a seven-day experiment and watch what changes.

At the end of each day, answer three questions in writing:

  • When did I use my phone without a clear purpose?
  • What was I trying to avoid, or what did I need at that moment?
  • What can I change tomorrow to respond more wisely?

Don’t turn the exercise into a reason for pride if you complete it, or into a reason for condemnation if you miss a day. The goal is to understand your own habits better and to make sustainable changes.

If a day doesn’t go well, pick the plan back up the next day. You don’t need a symbolic date to start again.

8. Spiritual exercise: examine the place your phone holds

Read 1 Corinthians 6:12 and Psalm 90:12 carefully.

Then write your answers to the following questions:

  • Is there a digital activity that I continue even when I notice it is affecting my responsibilities?
  • What important things do I repeatedly put off because of my phone?
  • What concrete change can I make to protect my prayer time, my study of Scripture, and my daily responsibilities?
  • Do I sometimes use my phone to avoid silence, discomfort, or a difficult conversation?
  • How can I use technology more consciously to learn, to serve, and to stay connected with people?

Don’t write the answers you think you should give. Write the truth you notice in your own life.

Prayer

Lord, search my heart and help me see honestly how I use my time and my attention. Show me where I have become inattentive, where I put off important things, and where I allow habits to drive my choices.

Give me wisdom to use technology without becoming dependent on its distractions. Teach me to pray sincerely, to read Scripture attentively, and to carry out my responsibilities faithfully.

Keep me from the pride that comes when I succeed and from the discouragement that comes when I fail. Help me not to seek merely the appearance of a spiritual life, but to grow in knowing You and in obeying Your Word.

Amen.

9. Questions for reflection

  • What is the difference between using your phone as a tool and coming under the power of a digital habit?
  • How can you apply the principle of 1 Corinthians 6:12 without declaring that all entertainment is sin?
  • Why is it important to change your environment and habits, not just to rely on willpower?
  • How can you tell the difference between consuming Christian content and applying biblical truth?
  • What concrete change will you keep over the next thirty days?

Conclusion: put the tool in its place

The phone can be a useful tool. It can support study, communication, work, and even spiritual growth. It should not be demonized, nor treated as if its mere existence were a moral problem.

But neither should we ignore the effect our habits can have.

If you say you want to know your Bible better, protect your study time. If you want to grow in prayer, give it a real place in your schedule. If you want to be present for the people around you, learn to put your phone aside. If you want to be a disciple of Jesus, don’t settle for consuming information about Him. Seek to follow His teaching.

You don’t need to prove that you can live without technology. You need to learn to use it with discernment.

And when you notice that a habit is taking the place that responsibilities, relationships, or your time with God should have, don’t stop at regret. Make a concrete decision and put it into practice.

Freedom does not mean being able to do anything at any moment. It also means being able to give up something permitted when that choice helps you stay faithful to what really matters.

In the next chapter we will go deeper: why we put off the very things we say we love, and how we can understand the difference between our intentions and the choices we make day after day.

Chapter 3 · Part I · The Diagnosis

Why Do You Put Off the Very Things You Say You Love?

“For the good which I desire, I don’t do; but the evil which I don’t desire, that I practice.” — Romans 7:19

There is a contradiction you have probably noticed in your own life.

You say you want to draw closer to God, but you put off prayer.

You say you want to know Scripture, but you always find something else to do.

You say you want to become more disciplined, but you repeat the same habits that keep you stuck.

In the evening you set goals, thinking about the person you would like to become. In the morning, though, you go back to the person you were yesterday.

You know what it would be good to do. Sometimes you even know how to do it. And still, you don’t do it.

Why?

Maybe the answer is not that you don’t care at all. Maybe you do care, but you haven’t learned to manage the gap between what you want in the long term and what gives you immediate satisfaction.

Maybe you have plans that are too ambitious, an unrealistic schedule, a habit of procrastination, or a tiredness you keep trying to ignore.

And sometimes there is a deeper problem: you like the idea of being a disciplined person more than you like the process by which discipline is formed.

This chapter is not written to make you feel guilty. It is written to help you understand what is happening and to begin changing things.

1. Wanting something doesn’t mean you are ready to practice it

Imagine two people.

The first says he wants to read the Bible every day. He buys a notebook, makes a plan, sets an ambitious goal, and talks enthusiastically about what he is going to do.

The second doesn’t talk much about his plans. Every morning, he opens Scripture, reads a passage, prays, and tries to apply what he has understood.

The first has an intention. The second has begun to turn intention into a habit.

There is nothing wrong with setting goals. We need them. The problem comes when we start to confuse planning with progress.

You can imagine a deep prayer life without praying. You can read about discipline without being disciplined. You can follow people who have achieved important things and feel, for a few minutes, that you too are getting closer to your goals.

But the feeling that you have done something is not always proof that you have done it.

Intention shows the direction you want to go. Action shows whether you have actually started walking.

In Matthew 7:24–27, Jesus compares the person who hears His words and does them to a wise man who builds his house on the rock. In contrast, the one who hears but doesn’t do them is compared to a man who builds on the sand.

The context matters: with this, Jesus closes a major part of His teaching in the Sermon on the Mount. He is not speaking only about productive habits, but about our response to His teaching.

The principle is simple: the truth we hear must become truth we practice.

You don’t just need a prettier plan for your spiritual life. You need to start doing what you have already understood.

2. Why do we choose immediate satisfaction over a greater good?

Prayer, Bible study, and character formation can have deep value, but they don’t always offer an immediate emotional reward.

Sometimes you pray and feel peace. At other times you are tired, your mind is restless, and you have to choose to remain before God even if you don’t feel anything spectacular.

Sometimes you read a Bible passage and immediately understand an important truth. At other times you need to read slowly, look into the context, and return to the text.

Digital entertainment, on the other hand, can offer novelty and immediate stimulation with very little effort.

Here an important difference appears:

  • The immediate reward: something pleasant, easily accessible, and available right now.
  • The long-term benefit: something valuable that takes time, patience, and consistency.

This difference shows up in many areas of life. A student may prefer entertainment to studying. An athlete may prefer comfort to training. A Christian may prefer to watch one more sermon instead of stopping to pray.

This does not mean that every easy choice is wrong or that you must always choose the hardest activity. It means you need to learn to recognize the moment when immediate comfort begins to run your life.

Proverbs 13:4 describes the contrast between the desire of the lazy person and the diligence of the hard worker. Biblical wisdom shows us that desire without action does not automatically produce the result we want.

Applied to the spiritual life, this means it is not enough to want to know God better. You have to make space to know His Word, to pray, and to live what you learn.

Don’t wait for every valuable thing to become easy or pleasant before you practice it.

Some habits become easier to sustain through repetition. Others remain demanding but are worth continuing for their value.

3. Sometimes you are not putting off the activity. You are putting off how you feel about it

Suppose you have decided to read a difficult chapter of the Bible.

You open the text and come across a passage you don’t understand. You begin to wonder whether you are interpreting it correctly. You realize you have to pay attention and can’t read in a hurry.

Then the urge to check your phone appears.

Maybe you aren’t necessarily looking for entertainment. Maybe you just want to escape the mental effort, the uncertainty, or the discomfort of not understanding right away.

The same thing can happen when you have to study for an exam, have a difficult conversation, or admit a mistake.

Sometimes procrastination is an attempt to avoid an unpleasant emotion.

You may be putting something off because:

  • the activity seems too big and you don’t know where to start;
  • you are afraid you won’t be able to do it well;
  • you are tired and need real rest;
  • you feel overwhelmed by everything you have to do;
  • you have associated the activity with pressure, guilt, or past failures.

These causes are not identical, and they are not all solved by the same method.

If the problem is tiredness, you need rest and a more realistic schedule. If the problem is perfectionism, you need to accept that you can start imperfectly. If the problem is a lack of clarity, you need to break the task into smaller steps.

That is why, the next time you put off prayer or study, don’t stop at the question: “Why am I so undisciplined?”

Ask yourself:

“What exactly is hard for me right now, and what am I trying to avoid?”

Maybe you will discover that you need to reduce distractions. Maybe you need to start with ten minutes, not an hour. Maybe you need to go to bed earlier. Or maybe you need to stop waiting for the perfect emotional state.

Understanding the problem does not replace responsibility, but it helps you choose a fitting solution.

4. The danger of unrealistic spiritual goals

Suppose that until now you have prayed only sporadically and read the Bible only occasionally.

One day, you are very motivated and decide that, starting tomorrow, you will spend three hours in prayer, two hours in Scripture, exercise daily, study more, and not waste a single minute.

It sounds impressive on paper.

The problem is that you have tried to change in a single day behaviors that were formed over time. If the plan doesn’t take into account your schedule, your rest, your responsibilities, and your current level of discipline, there is a risk that you won’t be able to sustain it.

After a few difficult days, you abandon the plan. Then you feel guilty and come to believe you are not capable of being consistent.

The cycle repeats: enthusiasm, excessive goals, exhaustion, abandonment, guilt, and a new spectacular beginning.

You don’t have to live like this.

If you want to reach long periods of prayer and Bible study, you can work toward it progressively. Start at a level you can sustain, then grow according to your schedule and responsibilities.

For example, if you currently pray very little, you can set a realistic daily slot and protect it. When it becomes sustainable, you can extend it.

If you have a busy day, you can adapt the plan without giving it up completely.

You don’t have to copy another Christian’s schedule. You have to build a faithful practice, suited to your responsibilities, that helps you grow.

In Luke 16:10, Jesus speaks about faithfulness in little things and in much. The context of the passage concerns stewardship and faithfulness, not a modern productivity system. Still, the principle invites us not to despise small steps taken seriously.

Don’t underestimate a modest practice that you keep up. And don’t confuse an impressive goal with a transformed life.

5. Condemnation is not the same as repentance

Here we have to make an essential distinction.

There is a difference between admitting that you have done wrong and concluding that there is no point in trying anymore.

Suppose you lost an hour on your phone even though you had meant to pray and study.

You can say: “I made a poor choice. I need to understand why it happened and change something.”

Or you can say: “I’m a hypocrite. I will never change. I have failed again.”

The first reaction can lead you to examination, responsibility, and change. The second risks keeping you stuck in shame.

We must not minimize sin, nor use grace as an excuse for a lack of responsibility. But neither should we turn every failure into a final verdict on our identity.

In 1 John 1:9, the apostle John speaks about confessing sins and about God’s faithfulness and justice to forgive and cleanse. In context, the message concerns sin, truth, and walking in the light.

It is not a promise that every habit disappears instantly after a prayer. It is a call to honesty before God and a testimony to His forgiveness.

When you fail:

  • Acknowledge what you did, without making excuses.
  • Confess before God what needs to be confessed.
  • Identify the circumstances that contributed to that choice.
  • Repair what you can repair and set a concrete change.
  • Resume the good practice without waiting for a new wave of enthusiasm.

Repentance does not mean punishing yourself emotionally until you feel you have suffered enough. It means turning back to God and responding to the truth with a real change of direction.

Grace does not make obedience unnecessary. Grace gives you the reason and the framework in which you can come before God honestly, without pretending to be faultless.

6. Don’t wait until you feel like it to begin

Feelings matter, but they are not always a sufficient guide for our decisions.

Sometimes you feel the desire to pray, and it comes easily to spend time in Scripture. At other times you are tired, distracted, or simply don’t feel the same enthusiasm.

If you make all your practices depend on your emotional state, your consistency will depend on factors that change.

This doesn’t mean you should ignore your body, your emotions, or your need for rest. It means learning to tell the difference between a real need for recovery and the tendency to put off every activity that requires effort.

You can use a simple rule:

Begin with a small step, then evaluate.

If you planned thirty minutes of Bible study, begin by reading the assigned passage and reflecting on it. You don’t have to feel an extraordinary desire beforehand.

If you planned prayer, stop the distractions and begin to pray sincerely, even if your mind is restless. If you notice that you are exhausted, adjust the length and also take care of the rest you need.

The goal is not to produce spiritual emotions on demand. The goal is to draw near to God sincerely and to form habits that support a life of faith.

In Luke 5:16 we read that Jesus withdrew to lonely places and prayed. The text presents prayer as part of His life, in the midst of ministry and the demands of the people.

We shouldn’t pretend that the passage gives a universal formula for a daily schedule. But it does show us the importance of a space set aside for prayer, even in the middle of an active life.

If Jesus made room for prayer, don’t treat your relationship with the Father as something worth attention only when you have nothing else left to do.

7. Build a system, not just a promise

A system is a set of decisions and conditions that helps you repeatedly do what you consider important.

A promise says: “Tomorrow I will read the Bible.”

A system answers concrete questions:

  • At what time will I read?
  • Where will I sit?
  • What passage will I read?
  • Where will I put my phone?
  • What will I do if my schedule changes?
  • How will I resume the practice after a missed day?

For example, you can decide that after your morning routine you will spend a period of time in prayer and Scripture, before opening any entertainment apps.

If your mornings are unpredictable, you can choose another time slot that you can realistically protect.

If you have military service, college, a job, or family responsibilities, the plan has to take them into account. Discipline does not mean ignoring real responsibilities in the name of an idealized schedule.

You can also use a backup plan:

  • The main plan: your usual time for prayer and study.
  • The backup plan: a shorter slot for unusually busy days.
  • The return plan: the next concrete opportunity when you resume your routine after an interruption.

The backup plan must not become a permanent pretext for doing the minimum. Its role is to help you keep continuity when real situations arise.

And one more thing: don’t turn spiritual discipline into a competition with other Christians.

You don’t have to pray more than someone else to prove your worth. You don’t have to read more chapters than your friend. You don’t have to post every spiritual practice so that someone can confirm you are serious.

Your relationship with God is not a leaderboard.

8. Practical exercise: from intention to action

Over the next three days, choose a single practice you have repeatedly put off. Don’t try to reorganize your whole life in one evening.

It can be Bible reading, prayer, studying for college, or another important responsibility.

Fill out the following exercise.

My consistency sheet

Fill it out realistically. It is a tool for you, not a spirituality test.

  1. What do I want to practice consistently?
  2. What does my situation look like right now?
  3. What makes me put it off?
  4. What is the concrete action?
  5. When will I do it?
  6. How much time will I set aside?
  7. How will I reduce the main obstacle?

At the end of the three days, evaluate what you actually did, not just how motivated you felt.

If the plan was too easy, you can extend it. If it was too difficult, identify the reason and adjust it. If you missed a day, don’t invent a punishment and don’t abandon the whole plan. Resume the practice at the next suitable opportunity.

The goal is to build consistency, not to get three perfect days.

9. Spiritual exercise: examine the gap between what you say and what you practice

Read James 1:22–25 and Matthew 7:24–27.

Then answer honestly:

  • What biblical truth have I known for some time but still not put into practice as I should?
  • What do I tell myself is important, yet repeatedly give too little time to?
  • Do I sometimes use planning, reading, or listening to sermons as a substitute for obedience?
  • What concrete change would be a practical response to the truth I know?
  • How can I make that change without falling into perfectionism or self-condemnation?

Don’t answer only with general goals such as “to be better” or “to pray more.”

Write down one observable action. For example: “For the next three days, I will read the assigned passage after my morning routine and note one concrete application.”

Prayer

Lord, help me to be honest with myself before You. Show me the difference between what I say is important and how I live my days.

Forgive me where I have known the truth but chosen to ignore it. Give me wisdom to understand the reasons I put things off, courage to take responsibility, and patience to build healthy habits.

Keep me from the pride of big promises and from the discouragement that comes when I fail to keep them. Teach me to respond to Your Word with sincere obedience and to return to You when I fail.

Help me not to seek merely the appearance of change, but to grow in faithfulness, character, and love for You and for the people around me.

Amen.

10. Questions for reflection

  • Why is it possible to sincerely want something and yet not act in its direction?
  • What is the difference between a lack of discipline, real tiredness, and avoiding discomfort?
  • How can a goal that is too ambitious contribute to a cycle of enthusiasm and abandonment?
  • Why must repentance not be confused with self-condemnation?
  • What simple system could help you turn an intention into a regular practice?
  • What is one biblical truth you can put into practice over the next 24 hours?

Conclusion: stop waiting for the perfect version of yourself

Maybe you have spent months imagining what a more disciplined life would look like. You made plans, started several times, and gave up when the enthusiasm disappeared.

You don’t have to stay stuck in that pattern.

You are not obliged to wait for a new season of motivation to begin. You don’t have to fix all your weaknesses before you take one good step. And you don’t have to pretend to be more disciplined than you really are.

Begin by telling the truth about your situation. Identify the obstacle. Choose one concrete step. Take it. Evaluate what happened and keep going.

In the Christian life, this approach does not replace God’s work, nor dependence on His grace. It is the way you take responsibility for your response to what you have understood from the Word.

You don’t need to feel ready for the whole journey. You need to respond faithfully to the step that is in front of you.

And in the next chapter we move from diagnosis to foundation: not just admiring Jesus, but following Him. Because the purpose of this book is not to turn you into an extremely productive person, but into a disciple who uses his time, his attention, and his life for what truly has value before God.

Chapter 4 · Part II · The Biblical Foundation

Not Just Admiring Jesus, but Following Him

“Whoever wants to come after me, let him deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me.” — Mark 8:34

1. You can know a lot about Jesus without following in His steps

We live in a time when you can listen to sermons almost anywhere. You can watch theological debates, study doctrines, listen to testimonies, save verses, and learn a great deal about the life of Jesus Christ.

All of this can be helpful.

But there is a question we must not avoid:

How much of what you know about Jesus shows in the way you live?

You can know the accounts about forgiveness and still refuse to forgive. You can talk about humility and get upset when you don’t receive the appreciation you want. You can listen to sermons about love and be indifferent to people who need help.

You can even be very busy with Christian activities but neglect the character God wants to form in you.

Here we have to make a distinction: admiring Jesus is not the same as being His disciple.

Admiration can stay at the level of emotions. Discipleship involves a relationship, a direction, and a concrete response to His teaching and authority.

This doesn’t mean emotions are useless. Love for God can involve joy, wonder, and gratitude. But emotions, however sincere, do not replace obedience.

In John 14:15, Jesus links love for Him to keeping His commandments. In the context of the discourse to the disciples before the crucifixion, He speaks about love, obedience, and the coming of the Comforter.

He is not saying that a person earns salvation through a string of moral achievements. Rather, He shows that love for Him cannot be separated from a real response to what He commands.

If you say you love Christ, it is worth asking yourself not only what you feel during worship, but also how you respond when His teaching confronts your choices.

2. What does it actually mean to be a disciple?

In the Gospels, Jesus does not call people merely to be admirers of His teaching. He calls them to come after Him.

In Mark 8:34, the call to discipleship includes three important expressions:

  • To deny oneself.
  • To take up one’s cross.
  • To follow Jesus.

The context is essential. Peter had just confessed Jesus as the Christ, but had reacted against the announcement that Jesus was going to suffer and die. Jesus then explains that His road to glory passes through suffering, and He calls those who follow Him to reconsider how they understand life, security, and gain.

So denying yourself does not mean hating yourself, ignoring your health, or giving up every personal responsibility.

It means no longer treating yourself as the supreme authority over your own life.

It means recognizing that your desires are not always right, that your plans may need to change, and that obeying Christ may require giving up something you want.

Taking up your cross does not mean that every daily inconvenience should be called a “cross” in the full sense of the passage. In the original context, the image of the cross was one of suffering and execution, and Jesus’ call was radical.

Applied to our lives, the call means a willingness to remain faithful to Him even when obedience has a cost.

And following Jesus means more than giving up things. It means walking in the direction He leads you.

It is not enough to say: “I don’t want to waste time anymore.”

You have to ask: “What do I want to use my time for?”

It is not enough to say: “I want to give up distractions.”

You have to ask: “What responsibilities, relationships, and forms of service do I want to cultivate?”

Discipleship is not only the elimination of bad things. It is also the building of a life directed toward what is good and true.

3. Why discipline without Christ is not the final goal

You can become a very disciplined person without being a disciple of Jesus.

You can wake up early, exercise, read a lot, work efficiently, and organize every hour. These can be good things. But taken on their own, they don’t prove that you love God or that you live in obedience to Him.

Discipline is a tool. It is not your Savior.

The purpose of this book is not to turn you into someone who checks off an impressive spiritual schedule. The purpose is to help you manage your life so that you can respond more consistently to the call of Christ.

That changes the question at the base of the whole process.

Instead of asking only:

“How can I wake up earlier?”

Ask also:

“How can I use the morning to draw near to God and carry out my responsibilities?”

Instead of asking only:

“How can I read five chapters a day?”

Ask also:

“How can I understand Scripture and respond with obedience to what I read?”

Instead of asking only:

“How can I eliminate all distractions?”

Ask also:

“How can I be more present before God and more attentive to the people He has put in my life?”

This is the difference between a life organized only around performance and a life organized around a calling.

In Philippians 3:7–14, Paul describes the change in his values in light of knowing Christ and his desire to know Him more deeply. He does not present the Christian life as a collection of achievements to be proud of, but as a race in which he keeps pressing on toward the goal of God’s calling.

This is not about indifference to effort. Paul speaks about running, perseverance, and pursuing a goal. But the goal is not personal glory.

Christian discipline does not aim to make you impressive in people’s eyes. It is meant to support your faithfulness to Christ.

4. Grace does not cancel obedience, and obedience does not buy grace

Here is a balance we have to keep.

On one hand, we must not turn prayer, Bible reading, and discipline into methods by which we try to earn God’s acceptance.

You are not more worthy of God’s love just because you read more chapters than yesterday. You cannot buy His favor with a certain number of hours of prayer.

On the other hand, we must not use grace as a justification for ignoring obedience.

The fact that salvation is by grace does not mean that how we live no longer matters.

In Ephesians 2:8–10, Paul explains that salvation is by grace, through faith, not by works, so that no one may boast. In verse 10, he goes on to say that we are created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand.

Notice the order of the argument:

  • Salvation is the gift of God’s grace.
  • A person has no reason to boast as if he had earned his salvation.
  • A transformed life nevertheless has a concrete direction, in which good works matter.

Good works are not the price you pay to be received by God. They are part of the life to which you are called in Christ.

This matters enormously for spiritual discipline.

If you pray only so that you feel good enough, prayer can become a source of pressure. If you read the Bible only to prove that you are a serious Christian, reading can become an exercise in comparison.

But if you draw near to God in response to His grace, prayer and Scripture become practices through which you seek to know Him, to hear His Word, and to grow in faith.

You don’t discipline yourself in order to force God to love you.

You organize your life because you want to respond faithfully to the One who calls you to follow Him.

5. What does discipleship look like on an ordinary day?

It is easy to imagine discipleship in extraordinary moments: a Christian conference, an evening of prayer, a powerful sermon, or a season in which we feel very close to God.

But most of life takes place on ordinary days.

You wake up. You have responsibilities. You have to study or work. You talk with people. You receive messages. You get tired. You make small choices, some of them without thinking much about them.

It is precisely in these moments that the direction of your life should become visible.

Discipleship can mean doing your studies seriously, without cheating. Speaking respectfully when you are irritated. Admitting a mistake instead of looking for an excuse. Keeping your promises. Stopping an activity that is taking your time in an unhealthy way. Helping someone without turning the gesture into a show.

It can also mean recognizing your limits, resting when you need to, and not neglecting your responsibilities to your family.

In Colossians 3:17, Paul urges believers to do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks through Him to God the Father. In context, the letter develops the implications of the new life in Christ for the behavior and relationships of believers.

The idea is not that every ordinary task must be turned into a religious ritual. Rather, the Christian life is not divided into a “spiritual” part, in which you follow Christ, and a completely separate part, in which it no longer matters how you live.

How you study, work, speak, and treat your neighbor is part of your moral response before God.

This means you cannot make up for a lack of integrity with many hours of prayer. Nor do you have to choose between closeness to God and your daily responsibilities, as if one were always spiritual and the other worthless.

You need a life in which both find their proper place.

6. The disciple’s three questions

When you don’t know how to organize your day, you can begin with three questions.

Question 1: What do I need to know from the Word of God?

You need Scripture to know the truth about God, about humanity, about sin, about salvation, and about the life to which you are called.

Don’t read just to check off a number of chapters. Read to understand the text in its context.

Sometimes you will read a short passage and need time to understand it. At other times you will be able to cover more. The important thing is not to confuse the speed of reading with the depth of understanding.

Question 2: What do I need to respond to with obedience?

After you have read, ask yourself what you have understood and what implications that truth has for your life.

Not every passage contains a direct command addressed to every reader. Some passages recount events, others present poetry, prophecy, or theological arguments. That is why the application must respect the meaning of the text, not invent arbitrary rules.

But when you understand a relevant biblical command or principle, don’t stop at mere admiration.

Seek to respond faithfully.

Question 3: Whom can I love and serve concretely today?

Discipleship is not only an inward activity.

In John 13:34–35, Jesus gives the disciples the command to love one another and shows that their love will be a distinguishing sign that they are His disciples.

The context is the last period Jesus spent with the disciples before the crucifixion, after He had washed their feet. The love He calls them to is not just a declared emotion, but an attitude that is expressed in their relationships with one another.

So don’t measure the day solely by how many things you read or how many minutes you spent in prayer.

Ask yourself also whether you were honest, whether you acted with integrity, whether you treated people with love, and whether you were willing to help when it was fitting.

These questions do not replace spiritual practices. They help you understand where those practices should lead you.

7. Practical exercise: a day organized around discipleship

For the next three days, don’t try to change every aspect of your life. Choose three markers that will help you live more consciously.

The three daily markers

Adapt them to your schedule. The goal is faithfulness, not a perfect day.

1. My relationship with God

Prayer and reading a Bible passage

2. My main responsibility

I carry out my main responsibility without distractions

3. Love and service

I give my attention to one person and look for a real opportunity to help

At the end of the day, I reflect

What did I understand, what did I practice, and what do I need to correct?

Daily review

Repeat the exercise for three days and observe what changes. If a marker doesn’t fit your schedule, adapt it. If you missed a day, return to the plan without turning a single failure into a conclusion about your whole spiritual life.

8. Spiritual exercise: examine your motivations

Read Mark 8:34–38 and Ephesians 2:8–10.

Then answer in writing:

  • Do I want to grow spiritually in order to know Christ better, or in order to feel superior to others?
  • Is there an area of my life where I admire Jesus’ teaching but avoid applying it?
  • Do I sometimes use discipline to prove my own worth?
  • What concrete responsibility requires faithfulness from me during this season?
  • How can my time with God help me love better and live more responsibly?

Don’t try to produce impressive answers. Honesty is more helpful than an answer that sounds spiritual but doesn’t reflect reality.

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, help me not to be content merely with knowing things about You, but to grow in faith, love, and obedience.

Keep me from the pride that sometimes hides behind discipline and from the tendency to measure my worth by my achievements. Help me understand Your grace and respond to that grace with a sincere life before You.

Teach me to use time wisely, to carry out my responsibilities with integrity, and to love the people around me through concrete actions.

When it is hard for me to obey, give me strength to do what is right. When I fail, help me return to You with repentance. When I succeed, keep me from pride.

Let my life be not just a collection of beautiful intentions, but a sincere response to Your call.

Amen.

9. Questions for reflection

  • What is the difference between admiring Jesus and being His disciple?
  • What does denying yourself mean in the context of Mark 8:34?
  • Why can’t personal discipline replace God’s grace?
  • How can discipleship be seen in your studies, your work, and your relationships?
  • What connection is there between love for Christ and obedience to His commands?
  • What concrete change can you make today in response to what you understand from Scripture?

Conclusion: not just a better schedule, but a better direction

You can organize your day to the minute and still lose sight of the purpose for which you live.

You can read a lot, work efficiently, and become very disciplined, but turn all these things into a personal self-improvement project.

That is why, before you build a better schedule, clarify your direction.

You don’t pursue discipline merely to have more control over your life. You don’t pursue prayer to accumulate an impressive number of hours. You don’t read the Bible so that you can say you have finished a reading plan.

You practice them in order to know God, to grow in faith, and to respond with obedience to the call of Christ.

You will have good days and difficult days. Sometimes you will be consistent, at other times you will discover that you need to correct something. The important thing is not to confuse progress with perfection, nor grace with indifference.

Don’t ask only whether you have become more productive. Ask whether the way you live your life increasingly reflects the truth you say you believe.

Chapter 5

The Secret of a Constant Prayer Life

You don’t just need moments when you feel like praying. You need to learn to come before God even when your feelings don’t push you in that direction.

There is a difference between believing in prayer and having a prayer life.

You can be convinced that prayer is important. You can know verses about prayer, listen to sermons about prayer, read books about prayer, and even tell others they need to pray more. And yet, when you honestly look at your schedule, you may discover that prayer takes up much less room than you thought.

Why?

Because considering something important does not automatically mean you will give it time.

This is one of the contradictions of modern man: we have time for the things that grab our attention, but we have to fight to make room for the things we truly consider important.

We check our phones without meaning to. We open an app without a clear reason. We lose whole minutes watching things we forget almost immediately. But when the time comes to pray, we begin to negotiate with ourselves.

“I’ll pray a little later.”

“Today I’m too tired.”

“Tomorrow I’ll get up earlier.”

“Tonight I’ll pray more to make up for it.”

And sometimes tomorrow looks strikingly like yesterday.

The problem is not always a lack of desire to seek God. Sometimes the problem is that our desire has not been turned into a concrete habit.

But before we talk about discipline, we need to understand something fundamental: prayer is not just an activity to add to your schedule. It is our response to God, who calls us to come before Him through Jesus Christ.

Discipline helps us be consistent. It does not buy God’s love, it does not make us deserve His grace, and it does not turn prayer into a method by which we force Him to do what we want.

The purpose of this chapter is not to make you check off more minutes of prayer. The purpose is to help you build a life in which you come before God regularly, sincerely, and with trust.

1. Jesus withdrew to pray

Let’s look at the example of Jesus.

In Luke 5:15–16, we notice that news about Him was spreading, crowds of people were coming to hear Him and be healed, and He withdrew to lonely places and prayed.

Notice the contrast.

There was a real need. People needed help. The crowds were seeking Him. His activity was growing. And yet the Gospel tells us that He withdrew to pray.

Jesus did not withdraw because people didn’t matter. He withdrew while He was working among people.

Here we find an important lesson: the needs around you will never completely disappear, so you can’t wait until you have finished absolutely everything before you begin to pray.

If you wait for a day when you have nothing to do, you may wait a very long time.

If you wait until you are never tired again, you will keep putting it off.

If you wait to feel an extraordinary desire for prayer every morning, you are building your discipline on an emotion that, by its nature, changes.

Jesus gives us an example of prioritizing prayer in the middle of an active life.

This doesn’t mean you have to mechanically copy every detail of His schedule. It means it is worth taking seriously the place prayer held in His life.

The question that matters

Don’t ask only: “How much do I want to pray?”

Also ask: “What concrete place have I made for prayer in my life?”

Desire matters. But if it isn’t accompanied by concrete decisions, it can stay at the level of intention for years.

Maybe you need to get up a little earlier. Maybe you need to give up your phone in the first minutes of the morning. Maybe you need to end the evening without one more episode or one more round of videos.

Not all of these changes are necessary for every person. But each of us has to identify what is eating up our time and decide what to change.

Exercise: Write down one thing that regularly takes the time you would like to use for prayer. Don’t condemn yourself for the answer. Identify it and decide what you will do differently.

2. Prayer is not a spiritual performance

In Matthew 6:5–13, Jesus speaks about prayer in the Sermon on the Mount.

He warns against praying to be seen by people and criticizes the idea that someone will be heard because of the many words spoken. Then He teaches the disciples how to pray, giving them the model known as the “Lord’s Prayer.”

The context is important: Jesus does not condemn public prayer, nor long prayers in themselves. He condemns hypocrisy and a mistaken trust in appearances or in the quantity of words.

In other words, you can spend a lot of time speaking religious words and still lose sight of the purpose of prayer.

Likewise, you can begin with a short, sincere prayer in which you confess a fear to God, thank Him for something, or ask Him for wisdom for a decision.

Length is not the only criterion of authenticity.

That doesn’t mean length doesn’t matter at all. If you want to grow in a relationship, you have to give it time. It is hard to cultivate closeness with someone you constantly ignore.

But time is a means by which you build consistency, not a score by which you measure your spiritual worth.

Two traps to avoid

The first trap: comparing yourself with others.

You hear that someone prays three hours a day and immediately conclude that you must do exactly the same. You try, you don’t manage, you get discouraged, and you quit.

The example of others can inspire you, but you shouldn’t automatically turn it into a universal measure of spiritual maturity.

The second trap: using grace as a justification for stagnation.

You tell yourself that God loves you anyway and that you don’t have to be perfect. The statement about grace is true, but the conclusion must not be that you can ignore prayer indefinitely.

Grace is not a reason to run from God. It is the reason you can come before Him even when you admit your weaknesses.

You don’t have to choose between discipline and grace. You need both, in their proper places.

3. How to begin when you don’t feel like it

Suppose you have decided to pray every morning.

On the first day you are motivated. You get your Bible ready, find a quiet place, and pray attentively.

On the second day it is a little harder.

On the third day you wake up tired.

On the fourth day you check your phone “for just two minutes” and discover that you have lost the time you had set aside for prayer.

What do you do?

First, don’t turn one missed morning into a sentence for the whole week.

You don’t have to say: “I’ve ruined everything, I’ll start on Monday.”

You can return the very same day.

Second, don’t wait for perfect motivation to appear. Learn to begin sincerely, even if your feelings aren’t spectacular.

You can simply say:

“Father, I am tired and my mind is scattered. I don’t want to pretend I am more attentive than I am. Help me come before You sincerely.”

This is not a magic formula. It is an example of a sincere prayer.

You don’t have to pretend before God. You can come before Him with your joy, your tiredness, your restlessness, and your questions.

In Philippians 4:6–7, Paul urges believers to bring their requests before God through prayer and thanksgiving. The text does not promise that every problem will disappear immediately or that you will automatically receive the result you want. It directs attention to trust in God and to His peace, which guards hearts and minds.

So don’t pray only when you feel that everything is going well. Bring to God also the things you don’t yet understand.

You don’t need a perfect state to begin to pray. You need sincerity and a willingness to come before Him.

4. Build a rhythm, not just an intention

One of the most practical ways to become consistent is to tie prayer to a relatively stable time and place.

Don’t rely only on the idea that you will pray “when I have time.”

On a busy day, free time doesn’t always appear by itself. Sometimes you have to reserve it in advance.

Try to settle three things:

  • The time: when you will usually pray.
  • The place: where you can concentrate with as few interruptions as possible.
  • The first step: what you will concretely do to begin.

For example:

  • After waking up, before opening social media, spend ten minutes in prayer.
  • During a break in the day, stop for a few minutes and talk with God about what you are going through.
  • In the evening, before bed, thank Him, confess where you have failed, and bring the next day before Him.

This is only a model, not a biblical command about the number or times of prayers.

If you have military service, classes, family responsibilities, or a demanding work rhythm, adapt the plan to reality. A good schedule is one that helps you be consistent, not one that looks impressive on paper and collapses after two days.

Start with a realistic goal

If right now you pray very rarely, you don’t have to start with two hours a day.

You can start with 10–15 minutes of attentive prayer, then grow gradually as you form your rhythm and learn to concentrate.

If you already have a stable prayer life, the next step may be to go deeper into the content of your prayer, to pray using Scripture, or to give more attention to interceding for others.

The goal is not to stay at the minimum permanently. The goal is to grow in a healthy way and not to confuse growth with haste.

5. What do you actually do during prayer?

Sometimes a person avoids prayer not only because he has no time. He also avoids it because he doesn’t know how to begin.

He sits down, closes his eyes, says a few sentences, and then his mind runs off to classes, problems, conversations, and things he has to do.

After a few minutes, he has the impression that he is no good at praying.

Prayer must not be turned into an elaborate speech. Still, a few markers can help you.

A simple model of prayer

1. Worship

Begin by directing your attention to God: who He is, His character, His holiness, His goodness, and His faithfulness.

Example: “Father, I want to turn my attention to You. Help me not to begin this day living only for my own concerns.”

2. Confession

Acknowledge before God your sin, wrong attitudes, and the things you need to set right.

Example: “Lord, I have been impatient and have spoken without love. Forgive me and help me repair what I did wrong.”

3. Thanksgiving

Remember the gifts and help you have received, including the ordinary things you have started to take for granted.

Example: “Thank You for the people You have put in my life, for the help I have received, and for the chance to do what is right today.”

4. Requests and intercession

Bring before God your own needs, those of your family, your church, and people who are going through trials.

Example: “Give me wisdom for today’s decisions. Help me love my family, carry out my responsibilities, and be attentive to the needs of those around me.”

These categories are not a law, nor the only possible order. They are a practical framework. You can follow the model of the prayer in Matthew 6, you can use the Psalms, or you can speak spontaneously with God.

The important thing is not to turn the model into a new mechanical routine.

You are not checking four boxes to get a spiritual result. You use these markers to order your attention and not get stuck in the same few requests.

When your mind wanders

Don’t panic, and don’t spend the next ten minutes condemning yourself for being distracted.

Notice the distraction, return to prayer, and continue.

If you remember an important responsibility, write it down on a sheet of paper and go back to what you were doing. If you are very tired, maybe you also need rest, not just more willpower.

Growing in discipline does not mean treating yourself like a machine that has to run perfectly. It means learning to return to what matters.

6. Let Scripture shape your prayer

Prayer and Bible reading can strengthen each other.

Sometimes you sit down to pray and don’t know what to say. At other times you read a Bible passage but close it without thinking about how it should influence your life.

You can join the two practices.

Suppose you are reading Psalm 23. Instead of rushing to finish the chapter, stop on the idea that the Lord is the Shepherd of the one who belongs to Him.

You can ask yourself:

  • What does this passage reveal to me about God?
  • What fear or worry could I bring before Him?
  • What truth does this text need to remind me of today?
  • Is there an attitude or an action I need to change?

Then respond in prayer.

If you read about forgiveness, pray for the power to forgive and the wisdom to act rightly. If you read about wisdom, ask God to help you make responsible decisions. If you read a rebuke, don’t immediately turn it into a message about everyone else’s mistakes. Let the text search you too.

Still, pay attention to context. Not every verse is an individual promise that you will receive exactly what you want. Read the passage in the light of all of Scripture and its message about God.

Prayer based on Scripture does not mean using biblical words to constrain God. It means letting your thinking, your desires, and your requests be shaped by His truth.

7. What does “Pray without ceasing” mean?

In 1 Thessalonians 5:17, Paul says: “Pray without ceasing.”

How should this command be understood?

Does it mean you must speak words of prayer every second, without sleeping, studying, working, or talking with people? No. Such an interpretation would ignore how a person lives and the wider context of biblical teaching.

The idea is a life marked by constant prayer and dependence on God, not just a few isolated religious moments.

You can pray in the morning, ask God for wisdom before a decision, thank Him during the day, pray for someone who comes to mind, and in the evening bring before Him what you have lived through.

You can pray quietly in a secluded place, but also in the middle of ordinary responsibilities.

These short moments don’t necessarily replace the periods set aside for prayer. They complete a rhythm in which God is not ignored for most of the day and sought only when a crisis appears.

Two dimensions of prayer

  • Dedicated prayer: a time set aside for worship, confession, thanksgiving, requests, and intercession.
  • Prayer throughout the day: moments when you speak with God about what you are going through and turn your attention to Him again.

Both can be part of a healthy prayer life.

You don’t have to choose between spending dedicated time in God’s presence and staying aware of Him in ordinary activities.

8. How to grow from a few minutes to a longer time of prayer

Maybe your goal is to reach an hour, two hours, or even more of prayer a day.

Such a goal can make sense in the context of a person’s calling, responsibilities, and season of life. But it must be pursued wisely.

Don’t turn the number of hours into a universal measure of faith. The Bible does not set a mandatory daily length for every Christian.

Moreover, time spent in prayer must not be a competition with your sleep, health, studies, family, or responsibilities. It is not proof of maturity to build a spiritual schedule that you can maintain only by neglecting your other duties.

If you want to grow, proceed gradually.

An example of four-week progress

WeekFocusWhat you do
Wk 1Establish the habitSet aside 10–15 minutes a day. Focus on consistency, not on the ideal length.
Wk 2Go deeperIf the first step is stable, add 5–10 minutes or a second short period during the day.
Wk 3Broaden the contentInclude intercession for others, prayer based on Scripture, and time to bring your real concerns to God.
Wk 4Evaluate and adjustObserve what works, what distracts you, and how much time you can realistically set aside without neglecting your responsibilities.

This is an example, not a mandatory calendar. If the rhythm is too demanding, adjust it. If you already have a stable habit, you can start from your current level.

The goal is to build a sustainable practice, not to win a temporary victory over the clock.

And one more thing: if one day you pray less than you planned, don’t automatically conclude that God is disappointed in you or that you have lost all your progress. Evaluate what happened, take your share of responsibility, and return to the rhythm you set.

9. When you feel nothing in prayer

This subject deserves to be treated seriously.

There are times when prayer comes naturally and the heart is full of gratitude and joy. There are also seasons when you pray without feeling any strong emotion.

Not every sincere prayer is accompanied by an intense emotional experience.

You don’t have to invent a state you don’t have. You don’t have to pretend to be full of joy if you are going through a hard season. And you must not conclude right away that God has abandoned you just because you don’t feel what you once felt.

The Psalms give us examples of people who approach God with trust, but also with questions, fear, pain, and bewilderment.

Sincere prayer can include hard questions. It can include weeping. It can include silence. Sometimes it can be a simple request for help.

Not every season of spiritual dryness has the same cause. Sometimes there is tiredness, stress, unresolved problems, or unrealistic expectations. At other times, we can’t identify a simple explanation.

It is not healthy to automatically diagnose every spiritual difficulty as a lack of faith.

In Romans 8:26, Paul speaks about the Spirit’s help in our weakness and about the fact that we don’t always know how we ought to pray. The passage doesn’t promise that we will always have the right words. It reminds us that our weakness is not an obstacle that God cannot meet with His help.

When you don’t know what to say, you can begin with the truth:

“Lord, I don’t know exactly how to pray right now. Help me stay sincere before You and take the next step I know.”

You don’t have to manufacture emotions in order to have an authentic spiritual life.

10. Prayer must also lead you to obedience

There is a subtle danger: using prayer as a refuge where we feel spiritual, while avoiding the concrete things we know we have to do.

You pray for good results on your exams, but refuse to study.

You pray for healthy relationships, but keep speaking disrespectfully.

You pray for more discipline, but let every impulse run your schedule.

You pray for people in need, but ignore real opportunities to help them.

Prayer is not a substitute for responsibility.

In James 1:22, believers are urged to be doers of the Word, not just hearers. In the context of this book, the principle is important: the spiritual life must not be reduced to what we hear, say, or feel.

Sometimes, after you pray for wisdom, the next step is to study and seek good counsel.

After you pray for reconciliation, maybe you need to ask for forgiveness.

After you pray for a more disciplined life, maybe you need to uninstall an app that needlessly consumes your time.

After you pray for someone, maybe you need to call him and listen.

This doesn’t mean you can control every outcome through personal effort. It means prayer and obedience must not be set against each other.

Pray as one who depends on God and act with the responsibility of one who has understood what he has to do.

11. Practical plan: the next seven days

Over the next week, don’t try to rebuild your whole life in one move. Focus on establishing a rhythm of prayer that you can keep going.

Prayer checklist

At the end of the seven days, don’t ask only how many days you checked off.

Ask yourself also:

  • Have I become more sincere before God?
  • What distraction most affected my prayer time?
  • Have I learned to return when my mind wandered?
  • Is there one thing I need to change in my schedule?
  • What can I continue next week without neglecting my responsibilities?

Your answers matter more than the impression that you carried out a program perfectly.

12. Questions for reflection

Set aside a few minutes to answer in writing.

  • What does my prayer life really look like, beyond what I say I want?
  • What makes me put off prayer most often: distractions, tiredness, lack of a schedule, discouragement, or something else?
  • Do I relate to prayer as a meeting with God or as an obligation I have to check off?
  • Have I compared myself with other Christians in a way that discouraged me or made me proud?
  • What concrete step can I take in the next 24 hours to set a better rhythm?
  • Is there a problem I pray about but, concerning which, I avoid a responsible action?
  • What would it mean for me to grow in consistency without turning my discipline into a source of condemnation?

Don’t try to write the answers that sound the most spiritual. Write the true answers. An honest diagnosis is more useful than a nice image of yourself.

Before the next chapter

A constant prayer life is not built by strong intentions alone. It is built through sincere closeness to God, through realistic discipline, and through returning to Him when you have drifted from the rhythm you set.

You don’t have to wait until you have a perfect schedule. You can begin with the time you have, in the situation you are in now.

But prayer is not the only practice you need to rebuild.

You can pray for wisdom and still need to learn to think biblically. You can ask God to change your life and still need to feed your mind with the truth of Scripture.

Chapter 6

How to Read the Bible So That You Understand It and Live It

The purpose of reading Scripture is not to finish as many chapters as possible, but to come to know God’s truth better and to let your life be shaped by it.

There is something you can do every morning that, at first glance, seems very spiritual: read five chapters of the Bible, close the book, and go on with your day remembering almost nothing of what you read.

You have met a goal. You have checked off an activity. You can even say that you read the Bible today.

But what really happened?

Did you understand the message of the passage? Did you notice what it says about God? Did you understand what the text means in its context? Did you identify something you need to believe, correct, or apply?

Or did you pass through the words while your mind was already on classes, on your phone, on the problems of the day, and on what you were going to do next?

I am not saying that every reading session has to produce an extraordinary revelation. Sometimes you read to become familiar with a book, to follow the thread of a narrative, or to form an overall picture. At other times a passage requires careful study and repeated return.

But you have to understand the difference between getting through the text and understanding its message.

The Bible is not a collection of motivational phrases from which we pick what we like. It is the Scripture through which God reveals Himself, and we must read it with attention, humility, and a desire to respond to the truth.

In the previous chapters you began to look at time and attention as resources you must manage wisely. You analyzed the role of the phone, of procrastination, of discipleship, and of prayer.

Now we need to talk about how you approach the Word of God.

Because it is not enough to free up your schedule for Bible reading. You also have to learn how to read.

1. The Bible is not a talisman, nor a simple self-help manual

Let’s begin with an important correction.

Sometimes Christians can end up treating the Bible as an object that has to be read so that the day will go well. They open the book, read a passage, and then feel they have fulfilled their spiritual obligation.

Others use it almost exclusively as a self-help manual: they look for verses about success, discipline, courage, leadership, or prosperity, and ignore the passages that confront their priorities.

Both approaches are incomplete.

The Bible teaches us about wisdom, work, relationships, character, and responsibility. But its message goes far deeper than that. It reveals to us who God is, the reality of sin, the need for redemption, the work of Jesus Christ, and the call to live in faith and obedience.

If you read only to find a sentence that will make your day easier, you risk turning Scripture into an instrument for your own goals.

Sometimes the text will encourage you.

At other times, it will correct you.

Sometimes it will give you a clear answer.

At other times, it will show you that you asked the wrong question or that you need to study more before drawing a conclusion.

You must not approach the Bible only with the question: “What does this passage offer me?”

Ask also: “What does this passage actually say, and what do I need to change in light of it?”

Why does this matter?

Because if you always choose only the texts that confirm your opinions, you no longer let Scripture correct you.

You end up reading the Bible to confirm your own ideas, not to test them.

A mature attitude is different: you come to the text with questions, but you are willing to revise your conclusions when you realize you were wrong.

Scripture must not constantly conform to your preferences. You have to learn to conform your thinking to its truth.

2. Begin with the question: “What does the text say?”

In 2 Timothy 3:16–17, Paul explains that all Scripture is inspired by God and is useful for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness, so that the man of God may be thoroughly equipped for every good work.

Notice that the text speaks about several effects of Scripture.

  • It teaches.
  • It reproves.
  • It corrects.
  • It trains.

In other words, the Word of God does not have only the role of comforting us. It forms us and corrects us.

But for this to happen, we must first understand what the text says.

Suppose you are reading Matthew 7:24–27, the passage about the wise man who builds his house on the rock and the foolish man who builds it on the sand.

A superficial application might be: “I have to be a strong person, build a stable career, and not let problems knock me down.”

You can find useful ideas about perseverance in this image, but that is not the central point of the passage.

In context, Jesus closes a large section of teaching by saying that the wise person is the one who hears His words and does them. The issue is not only whether you have a solid foundation in a general sense, but whether you respond with obedience to what Jesus says.

If you remove the context, you can turn almost any passage into a lesson about any subject you like.

That is why the first rule of Bible study is simple:

Before you ask what the text means for you, try to understand what the text communicated in its own context.

Three basic questions

When you read a passage, stop and ask yourself:

  • What does the text actually say?
  • What does it mean in the context of the chapter and the book?
  • What principle or truth legitimately follows from the passage?

Only after that do you move on to personal application.

This order protects you from invented interpretations, hasty conclusions, and the use of verses to support ideas the text does not affirm.

3. Learn to read the context, not just the verse

A verse taken out of context can end up being used to support almost anything.

Let’s take Philippians 4:13: “I can do all things through Christ, who strengthens me.”

The verse is often used as a general declaration about reaching any personal goal.

But if you read Philippians 4:10–13, you notice that Paul is talking about having learned to be content in different circumstances, both in need and in abundance. He is talking about the strength he receives from Christ to remain steadfast in the situations he goes through.

This doesn’t mean a Christian shouldn’t have goals or work toward them. It means we must not turn the verse into a promise that we will obtain any result we want if we have enough faith.

Context changes how we understand the text.

The same principle applies when you read promises, commands, parables, or prophetic passages.

A simple exercise

When you come across a verse that catches your attention:

  • Read the verses before and after it.
  • Identify the people it is addressed to.
  • Notice the problem or situation being discussed.
  • Read the whole chapter, if you can.
  • Check whether your interpretation fits the rest of the passage.

If the text is difficult, don’t rush to form a conclusion just because it sounds profound.

It is perfectly acceptable to write in your notebook: “I don’t yet fully understand this passage.”

This is not a defeat. It is the beginning of serious study.

4. The four questions that help you study a passage

You don’t need a complicated system for every reading session. You need a few good questions that you learn to use consistently.

Question 1: What do I observe?

Look carefully at the text before you interpret it. Who is speaking? Who is being addressed? Are there commands, promises, warnings, repetitions, contrasts, or a sequence of events?

At this stage, try not to jump straight to personal application.

Question 2: What does it mean?

Read the passage in its historical, literary, and biblical context. What problem is the author addressing? What would the first recipients have understood? What words or ideas need clarification?

If necessary, consult an additional translation or a trustworthy Bible commentary.

Question 3: What does it teach me about God and about humanity?

Notice what the passage reveals about God’s character, His work, the human condition, sin, faith, grace, or obedience.

Don’t force every verse to answer all of these themes. Let the passage deliver its own message.

Question 4: How should I respond?

Is there a truth I need to believe, a sin to confess, a command to obey, an attitude to change, or a promise to put my trust in?

Apply the text in a concrete way, but one that respects its meaning.

These four questions can turn hurried reading into a much more careful study.

You don’t have to write whole pages every day. Sometimes a two-sentence answer is enough. The important thing is to learn to think while you read.

5. Don’t read the Bible only to find something about yourself. Seek to know God.

This distinction is essential.

You can read a biblical story and immediately ask: “How can I be like David? How can I have his courage? How can I win my battles?”

Sometimes we can learn important principles from the example of biblical characters. But if this becomes our only method, we risk reducing Scripture to a collection of moral stories.

Let’s take the example of David and Goliath in 1 Samuel 17.

A superficial reading might say: “My Goliath is any big problem in my life. If I have enough courage, I will defeat it.”

But the passage is not a promise that every personal obstacle will be removed the way we want. In the narrative, David puts his trust in the Lord, the God of Israel, and the confrontation takes place in a concrete context, tied to the Philistine threat and the history of the people of Israel.

You can learn from the passage about trusting God and about courage that doesn’t rest only on appearances. But you must first respect what the story says.

Moreover, when we read the whole of Scripture in the light of the work of Jesus Christ, we must avoid two extremes:

  • Ignoring how each passage contributes to the broader biblical message.
  • Turning every detail into a hidden symbol about Jesus, with no support in the text.

A Christ-centered reading does not mean inventing spiritual meanings for every object or number. It means understanding Scripture as a whole, including how Jesus and the apostles explain the fulfillment of God’s plan.

In Luke 24:27, after the resurrection, Jesus explains to the two disciples the things concerning Himself in the Scriptures, beginning with Moses and all the prophets. This passage shows us that the Scriptures must be read in light of God’s plan fulfilled in Christ.

But it does not justify arbitrary interpretations.

The goal is not to find a secret code in every verse, but to better understand God’s revelation and the place of the passage in the message of all Scripture.

6. How to read different kinds of biblical texts

Not all passages should be read in exactly the same way. The Bible contains narratives, poetry, wisdom, prophecy, Gospels, letters, and apocalyptic texts.

If you ignore the literary genre, you can end up with wrong interpretations.

Historical narratives

Examples: Genesis, Exodus, 1 Samuel, Acts

Follow the characters, events, conflict, and consequences. The fact that the Bible reports an action does not automatically mean it approves of that action. Sometimes the text describes sins and failures precisely to show their reality.

Poetry and Psalms

Examples: the Psalms, many passages in Job

Pay attention to images, metaphors, repetitions, and emotions. A poetic expression must not always be interpreted literally. The Psalms teach us even how to bring joy, fear, repentance, and pain before God.

Wisdom literature

Examples: Proverbs, Ecclesiastes

Notice the type of statement. Proverbs generally offer principles of wisdom, not mechanical guarantees that every situation in life will follow the same rule without exception. Ecclesiastes also explores the limits of human experience.

Prophets

Examples: Isaiah, Jeremiah, Amos

Read the message in the historical context of the people, the covenant, judgment, and hope. Don’t automatically assume that every prophecy refers directly to present-day events.

Gospels

Matthew, Mark, Luke, John

Follow who Jesus is, what He proclaims, what He does, how people respond, and what His death and resurrection mean. Also notice the context of His teachings.

Letters

Examples: Romans, Ephesians, James

Follow the author’s argument. Why does he affirm that? What question or problem is he answering? How do the practical commands connect with the teaching presented earlier?

Apocalyptic literature

Examples: Daniel 7–12, Revelation

Pay attention to symbols, images, and connections with earlier prophets. Avoid assigning a contemporary meaning to every symbol without solid arguments from the text.

You don’t have to become a specialist in all these genres before you can read the Bible. But a basic understanding of the differences between them helps you read more responsibly.

7. What do you do when you come across a difficult verse?

Suppose you are reading a passage and don’t understand it.

You have two options.

The first: you immediately choose the interpretation you like best and move on.

The second: you accept that you have run into a difficulty and begin to investigate it.

The second option requires more patience, but it helps you grow in understanding.

You can follow these steps:

  • Read the passage several times.
  • Read the broader context.
  • Identify unclear terms, expressions, or ideas.
  • Compare trustworthy Bible translations, if helpful.
  • Consult study notes, Bible dictionaries, or serious commentaries.
  • Compare the proposed interpretation with other relevant passages, without ignoring differences of context.
  • If there are several well-argued Christian interpretations, note them and examine the reasons for each.

A study tool can be helpful, but it is not infallible just because it looks professional or because the author is well known.

The same is true of explanations found online or generated with the help of artificial intelligence. They can offer a starting point, but they must be checked against the biblical text and trustworthy sources.

An important rule

Don’t confuse an interesting explanation with a true one.

A Greek or Hebrew word can have several meanings depending on context. It is not enough to find out that a term has a certain meaning in a dictionary and then claim that all possible nuances are present in the verse you are studying.

Likewise, you must not turn every seemingly minor detail into a hidden revelation.

Sometimes the correct explanation is simpler than the spectacular one.

Spiritual maturity also involves the ability to say: “I don’t know yet.”

8. How to organize your time for Bible reading

Now let’s move on to the practical part.

If your goal is to read the Bible daily, you need to set a rhythm that fits your real life.

There is no single mandatory schedule for all Christians. A student with a demanding schedule, a parent caring for small children, and a person with a flexible schedule may have different needs.

The principle is to be consistent without becoming rigid.

Don’t let perfectionism become another form of procrastination, either.

How do you reach an hour of reading and study?

If you want to spend an hour a day in Scripture, you can combine several activities:

  • continuous reading of a biblical book;
  • careful study of a passage;
  • re-reading important texts;
  • writing down questions;
  • consulting study tools;
  • prayer in response to the text.

You don’t have to spend every minute looking for additional explanations. Sometimes it is better to read the following chapters to understand the whole argument than to stay stuck for hours on a single word.

If you want to grow toward two or three hours of Bible study a day, think of this goal as a broad practice of reading, study, reflection, and prayer, adapted to your responsibilities. It is not a universal requirement for being a mature Christian.

9. Which book of the Bible should you read first?

Many people begin reading the Bible from Genesis and plan to continue through Revelation.

It is a valid approach, especially for someone who wants to understand the whole biblical narrative. Still, some get stuck when they reach legal passages, genealogies, or texts they don’t yet know how to interpret.

If you are at the beginning of a consistent routine, you can start with one of the Gospels, then continue with other books.

Mark

A good choice if you want a relatively concise and dynamic presentation of the work of Jesus, His disciples, and the road to the cross.

John

Suitable if you want to study more closely the identity of Jesus, His signs, His relationship with the Father, and the call to faith.

Ephesians

A useful book for studying grace, the work of Christ, the identity of believers, and how faith must be reflected in the life of the community.

Psalms

Very useful for learning the language of prayer, trust, repentance, praise, and lament. Read them keeping in mind their poetic character and context.

There is no single order that every Christian must follow.

If you have already started reading a book, it is often more useful to continue than to change the plan every week because you have discovered a new study method.

Consistency in a good-enough plan is often more helpful than an endless search for the perfect plan.

10. How to use a Bible study notebook

A notebook can become a simple and valuable tool. It doesn’t have to be artistic, have ten colors, or look like the pages you see on social media.

Its role is to help you observe, think, and return to what you have learned.

For each session, you can write down five things:

  1. The passage readExample: Mark 1:35–39.
  2. The central ideaSummarize the passage in one or two sentences, without adding ideas that aren’t there.
  3. What did I understand about God?Write down what the text actually shows, not just what you felt while reading it.
  4. What questions do I have?Write what you don’t understand and check it later, without inventing an answer to fill the page.
  5. How will I respond?Identify a concrete application, fitting the meaning of the passage.

Let’s apply the method to Mark 1:35–39.

In the passage, Jesus withdraws to a lonely place to pray. Simon and the others look for Him and tell Him that people are seeking Him. Jesus answers by speaking about going to other places to preach.

One possible observation: prayer appears in the middle of an active ministry, and Jesus does not let His whole schedule be determined solely by the pressure of the people around Him.

A question: what role does withdrawing to pray play in this sequence?

A careful application: I can set aside time for prayer and evaluate my priorities, without assuming that every detail of Jesus’ schedule must be copied exactly.

Notice the difference: the application comes from the passage, but it does not claim that the passage provides a universal daily schedule.

11. Don’t confuse accumulating information with transformation

You can know a lot of biblical information and remain weak in obedience.

You can memorize dozens of verses and continue to treat your family without patience.

You can attend Bible studies and refuse to admit your mistakes.

You can explain correct doctrines and use your knowledge to look down on those who know less.

These things don’t mean that in-depth study is useless. On the contrary, correct knowledge matters. The problem arises when the accumulation of information is confused with spiritual maturity.

In James 1:22–25, the author uses the image of a man who looks at himself in a mirror, walks away, and forgets what he looks like. He warns against hearing the Word without doing it.

The image challenges us to examine ourselves.

What good is it to recognize from Scripture that you must forgive, if you deliberately refuse to take steps toward forgiveness?

What good is it to study the truth, if you choose to lie when the truth costs you?

What good is it to read about service, if you build your whole life around your own comfort?

Application doesn’t mean that every passage must immediately be turned into a task list. Some texts call you to understand, to contemplate, to wait, or to go deeper. But reading Scripture must be open to transformation, not just accumulation.

A question for every day

At the end of your reading, ask yourself:

“If I take seriously what I understood today, what should be different in how I think, speak, or act?”

Don’t always look for a spectacular change.

Sometimes the answer is to stop spreading unverified information. At other times, it is to ask forgiveness, do your work with integrity, or give up a habit that is affecting your relationships.

Faithfulness is often seen in ordinary things.

12. Practical plan: seven days of attentive reading

This week, the goal is not to read as many chapters as possible. It is to practice a method you can keep using after you finish this chapter.

The seven-day journal

For each passage, answer the following in your notebook:

  • What do I observe in the text?
  • What does it mean in context?
  • What do I learn about God and about humanity?
  • How should I respond?

If you don’t understand something, write down the question and keep going. You don’t have to resolve every difficulty before you can read the next passage.

At the end of the week, re-read your notes. See whether you have become more attentive to context, whether you have formulated better questions, and whether you can explain the central idea of each passage without relying only on an impression.

13. Questions for reflection

Answer honestly, without writing what you think a disciplined Christian ought to say.

  • When I read the Bible, am I attentive to the text, or do I rush to finish the number of chapters I set?
  • Do I have a habit of taking verses out of context to support my ideas?
  • What do I do when I come across a passage I don’t understand?
  • Do I choose my reading only from the passages that encourage me, or am I willing to read the texts that confront me too?
  • What difference is there between what I know from Scripture and what I actually practice?
  • What simple method will I use over the next seven days to read more attentively?
  • Is there a concrete change I have kept putting off, even though I already understand what I have to do?

Don’t be discouraged if the answers reveal gaps. That is exactly why you study.

The goal is not to prove that you already know everything. The goal is to grow in understanding, discernment, and obedience.

Chapter 7

Build Your Day Before the Algorithms Build It for You

If you don’t decide in advance what deserves your attention, someone else will try to decide for you.

Imagine two mornings.

In the first, you wake up, pick up your phone, and check your notifications. You see a message, reply, then open an app. A video catches your eye. After it, another one appears. You remember that you were supposed to pray, but first you check what time it is. Then you tell yourself you will make up the time later.

By the time you finally get out of bed, the day has already started, but you haven’t decided what you will do with it.

In the second morning, you wake up and already know the first things you will do. You put your phone aside, pray, read Scripture, get your things ready, and begin the important activities. Not because you have suddenly become a person without weaknesses, but because you made a few decisions before the distractions began.

The difference between the two mornings is not necessarily motivation. It is structure.

The first day is driven largely by impulses and outside stimuli. The second begins with a clear intention.

Of course, real life doesn’t always follow the schedule. Emergencies come up, people need help, responsibilities appear unexpectedly, and there are days when you are more tired than you anticipated.

The goal is not to control every minute. The goal is not to live permanently in reactive mode, responding to whatever demands your attention without asking whether it deserves to receive it.

In the earlier chapters you analyzed time, attention, the phone, prayer, and the reading of Scripture. Now you have to place them in a practical system.

Because a disciplined life isn’t built only from decisions made in moments when you feel motivated. It is also built from how you organize your environment, your schedule, and your priorities.

1. Not everything urgent is important

The phone has an extraordinary capacity to make unimportant things seem urgent.

A notification appears on the screen. You feel the impulse to check it. A message interrupts your concentration. An app shows you that there is new content. People post, comment, share, and react.

All of this gives you the sense that something is happening that you must take part in.

But ask yourself: do you really have to take part?

If a friend posts a photo, do you have to see it within the first thirty seconds?

If an interesting video appears, do you have to watch it before you finish your studying?

If the phone vibrates while you are praying, are you obliged to interrupt your prayer?

Sometimes, yes, there may be an urgent situation. But most of the time, the things that interrupt us aren’t as urgent as they seem.

The problem is that we have come to confuse constant availability with responsibility.

You don’t have to reply instantly to every message in order to be a good friend. You don’t have to check every piece of news in order to be informed. You don’t have to be permanently connected in order to be present in people’s lives.

You can be responsible without being available every second.

Four categories for your time

Try to look at your activities through the following categories:

1. Important and urgent

Examples: a real family problem, a task with an approaching deadline, a responsibility that cannot be put off.

It requires immediate attention or a quick decision.

2. Important, but not urgent

Examples: prayer, study, character development, exercise, relationships, and preparing for future exams.

These things need to be scheduled before emergencies take up all the space.

3. Urgent in appearance

Examples: notifications, messages that can wait, updates, and requests that don’t call for an immediate response.

Learn to decide when you will check them, instead of reacting automatically.

4. Neither important nor urgent

Examples: aimless scrolling, repeatedly checking the same apps, and consuming content you didn’t even intend to watch.

Reduce these activities when they consume the time you need for important things.

This is a practical adaptation of the well-known prioritization matrix from time management. It is not a strictly biblical classification, but it can help you apply the principles of responsibility and wisdom.

Notice the second category: things that are important but don’t seem urgent.

This is where prayer, Bible reading, consistent study, sincere conversations with family, and the development of good habits often end up.

Nobody forces you to do them right this second. That is exactly why it is easy to put them off.

But the fact that something can wait doesn’t mean it should be put off indefinitely.

2. What does the Bible teach us about managing time?

In Ephesians 5:15–17, Paul urges believers to walk carefully, not as unwise people but as wise, redeeming the time because the days are evil, and to understand what the will of the Lord is.

The passage doesn’t give us a modern productivity technique. It doesn’t talk about digital calendars, organizing apps, or to-do lists.

But it gives us a deeper principle: how we live our time must be shaped by wisdom and by understanding the will of God.

Redeeming the time doesn’t mean filling every minute with religious or productive activities. It means not treating time as a resource without moral value.

Your time is where your real life takes place.

In it, you love your family or neglect it. In it, you study or put things off. In it, you pray or always leave prayer for later. In it, you serve people or focus exclusively on your own desires.

Not every minute has to produce a measurable result. Rest, play, friendship, and recreation also have a legitimate place. The problem appears when activities without a clear purpose repeatedly take the space of the things you consider essential.

A necessary distinction

There is a difference between being busy and being faithful to your responsibilities.

You can have a very crowded day and still avoid the main task.

You can spend hours organizing your schedule and never actually start the work.

You can follow content about discipline without applying anything you have learned.

Even organizing can become an elegant form of procrastination. People always find creative ways to avoid important work, and now they have specialized apps for it.

So don’t ask only how much you have done. Ask also whether you have done what mattered.

3. Don’t build your schedule around your phone

Let’s do a simple exercise.

Think about the first hour after you wake up.

If the first thing you do is open your phone, your attention immediately goes to other people’s content. You may see what happened overnight, what your friends have posted, what news has appeared, and what the algorithm recommends.

Before you have chosen what to focus on, you have already begun reacting to outside stimuli.

It doesn’t mean that every use of the phone in the morning is wrong. Maybe you need the alarm, an important message, or some information for your work.

The question is whether you use the phone intentionally or whether its mere presence dictates your behavior.

Create a rule for the first hour

You can try the following order:

  • You get out of bed and take care of your basic needs.
  • You pray and read Scripture, according to a realistic plan.
  • You check your schedule and identify the day’s responsibilities.
  • Only then do you open the apps that aren’t needed right away.

If you can’t reserve a whole hour in the morning, don’t turn the rule into an obstacle. You can begin with the first 15–20 minutes without social media.

The idea is not to prove that you can stay away from your phone as in a competition. The idea is to begin your day with intention.

Change the environment, not just the promises

If the phone is next to your pillow, within reach, every impulse to check it is easy to follow.

If you leave it at a distance, turn off non-essential notifications, and prepare your Bible the night before, the desired behavior becomes easier.

These are small changes to the environment. They don’t replace character or willpower, but they reduce the number of moments when you have to fight the same temptation.

Don’t rely exclusively on the power to resist a distraction. Organize your life so that the distraction has fewer opportunities to lead you.

4. Plan priorities before hours

One of the common mistakes in organizing a day is to start with the hours and try to fill every free slot.

A better method is to start with priorities.

First you identify what matters. Then you decide where it can be placed in your schedule.

For example, if you want to be consistent in prayer, Bible reading, and study, it isn’t enough to write “prayer, Bible, study” in your planner. You have to decide when you will do them and what you will cut back to make room for them.

Choose three daily priorities

Every evening, write down three important things for the next day.

Not twelve. Not a list so long that you feel defeated before you begin.

Three main priorities are a useful starting point.

For example:

  • Spiritual: a time dedicated to prayer and reading Scripture.
  • Responsibility: preparing for an exam or finishing a task.
  • Relationships and health: an attentive conversation with someone dear, exercise, or adequate rest, depending on the needs of the day.

These categories don’t have to appear in the same order every day. If there is a real emergency, the schedule adapts.

The important thing is not to confuse priorities with an exhaustive list of everything you would like to do.

A to-do list can contain twenty items. But your day also has to have a hierarchy.

Ask yourself in the evening

  • What important thing am I avoiding?
  • What absolutely has to be done tomorrow?
  • What can I put off without serious consequences?
  • What activity consumes my time without giving me a real benefit?
  • What do I need to prepare today so that tomorrow starts more easily?

Answer, and then finish planning. You don’t need to spend half an hour perfecting a list that you will abandon in the morning.

5. Build a realistic schedule, not an idealized one

Suppose you want a day in which you pray for two hours, read the Bible for an hour, study for four hours, exercise, carry out your responsibilities, spend time with your family, and sleep enough.

All these goals can have value. But there has to be real time for them.

If you plan more activities than fit into your day, you haven’t built an ambitious schedule. You have built an impossible one.

And if every evening you conclude that you have failed, even though the problem was the unrealistic plan, you risk losing confidence in your own ability to be consistent.

Plan on three levels

LevelTypeWhat it includes
Level 1ObligatoryThe things that have to be done: classes, work, family responsibilities, sleep, tasks with deadlines.
Level 2Growth prioritiesPrayer, Scripture, additional study, physical exercise, and the development of important habits.
Level 3Flexible and optionalRecreational activities, extra projects, non-urgent reading, and other useful things that can be moved when the day is crowded.

This classification doesn’t mean that rest or time with family are unimportant. On the contrary, you must include them in your schedule consciously, not treat them as the leftovers of the day.

Also, you must not use planning to justify neglecting sleep or health. Rest is part of a responsible rhythm of life.

In Mark 6:31, Jesus tells the disciples to come away to a deserted place and rest a while. The context shows that so many people were coming and going that the disciples did not even have time to eat.

The passage is not an energy management manual, but it shows that rest has a legitimate place even in the middle of important work.

You don’t have to choose between laziness and exhaustion. There is also the option of responsibility supported by a healthy rhythm.

6. Learn to work in focus blocks

Multitasking seems efficient, but repeatedly switching between activities can fragment your attention.

You start reading some material, check a message, go back to the material, look something up online, and then open another app. In the end, you have the impression that you were busy the whole time, but it is hard to say what you understood.

For tasks that require concentration, try working in dedicated intervals.

The basic method

  • Choose a single task.
  • Set an interval of 25–45 minutes, depending on difficulty.
  • Put your phone aside and close the tabs you don’t need.
  • Work only on the chosen task.
  • Take a short break and decide whether you need another interval.

If you are a beginner, you can start with 15 minutes of concentration and increase gradually.

There is nothing magic about the number 25. The interval must fit the task and your current capacity for concentration.

For Bible reading, you can have an interval in which you read and study attentively, without checking your phone every few minutes.

For study, you can work on a single subject until you finish a clear section.

For conversations with people, you can put your phone aside and be present.

What do you do when you remember something else?

Keep a sheet of paper next to you to write down ideas or tasks that come to mind.

If, while you are studying, you remember that you need to send a message, write “send the message after the session” and keep going.

Not every thought that appears has to be followed immediately.

Sometimes discipline means being able to say: “I noticed this. I will deal with it at the right time.”

7. Don’t let algorithms decide what is worth learning

The phone is not just a device you use for communication. In many apps, it is also a gateway to systems that select and recommend content based on a variety of signals about the user’s activity.

Their purpose and functioning differ from one platform to another, but recommendation feeds can encourage you to keep consuming content.

You watch a video about personal development and get another one. Then a clip about spiritual discipline appears. After it, one about financial success. Then a sermon excerpt, a controversy, and a reaction to the controversy.

In the end, you have consumed a multitude of ideas, but you may not have put any of them into practice.

Here an essential distinction appears: consuming content about an activity is not the same as practicing the activity.

Watching ten videos about prayer is not the same as praying.

Following a course about learning is not the same as studying.

Listening to a sermon about love is not the same as treating a difficult person with love.

Content can be useful. The problem appears when consuming it becomes a substitute for action.

The rule: learn, apply, then continue

When you come across a helpful idea, ask yourself:

  • What did I learn concretely?
  • How can I verify that the idea is correct?
  • What can I apply today?
  • Do I need more content, or do I need to put into practice what I already know?

You don’t have to give up all educational videos or all social media. You have to use them with intention.

If a resource helps you understand a concept, apply it. If it only makes you feel that you are making progress, without changing anything in your behavior, reevaluate how much time you give it.

8. Make room for people, not just goals

There is a danger in any book about discipline: making you believe that a good day is a day in which you completed all your tasks.

But people are not tasks.

Sometimes a friend needs you to listen to him, even if it changes your schedule. A family member may need help. A person may be going through a hard time and need your presence, not a speech about efficiency.

You don’t have to respond to every request at every moment. You also need limits. But neither should you become so preoccupied with your own schedule that you no longer notice the people around you.

In Luke 10:38–42, Martha is distracted with many tasks, while Mary sits and listens to Jesus’ teaching. Jesus draws Martha’s attention to her worry and agitation and emphasizes the importance of Mary’s choice.

The passage doesn’t say that all practical responsibilities are wrong. Nor does it justify neglecting all tasks in the name of an apparent spirituality. It confronts us with the danger of being so preoccupied with many things that we lose sight of what is essential.

Sometimes you have to put your phone away in order to pray.

At other times, you have to close your planner in order to listen to a person.

And sometimes you have to give up an extra activity in order to rest.

True discipline doesn’t turn you into a robot that carries out its schedule regardless of context. It helps you be more faithful to the things that matter, including when they cannot be measured by a checklist.

9. Plan your day around your energy

Not all hours of a day are the same.

Maybe you concentrate better in the morning. Maybe you have classes that demand your attention and you need a transition period after them. Maybe in the evening you are too tired for difficult study, but you can do a light review.

Instead of building your schedule only around the available hours, also notice when you function best.

A practical principle

Put the activities that require the most concentration in the periods when you are usually more alert, if your schedule allows it.

For example:

  • In a good period of concentration, work on the most difficult subject.
  • In periods of lower energy, do administrative tasks or reviews.
  • Reserve time for rest and recreational activities, instead of waiting until you can’t continue.

For prayer and Scripture reading, you can choose times when you are more likely to be attentive. Some prefer the morning; others need a different rhythm because of their responsibilities.

There is no magic hour that works for everyone.

If the morning is the only period when you have quiet, protect it. If you have a schedule that makes this impossible, find another stable time.

The principle is not to let important activities be permanently pushed into the intervals when you no longer have energy.

10. What do you do when the day doesn’t go according to plan?

Suppose you have prepared a good schedule.

You got up on time, read Scripture, started studying, and then received unexpected news. Or you had a much more demanding day than you thought. Or you simply couldn’t concentrate.

What do you do?

First, distinguish between a real obstacle and a distraction you could have avoided.

Not all interruptions are your fault. But neither should you call every impulse an emergency.

Second, adapt the plan.

If you can’t do everything, identify what is essential and what can be moved. If you missed a study period, maybe you can make up part of it later without sacrificing your sleep.

Third, don’t turn an imperfect day into an abandoned week.

A missed day doesn’t cancel all your previous progress.

You can return to the next scheduled activity. You don’t have to wait for Monday, the start of the month, or a new period of motivation.

Avoid two extremes

Rigidity: you cling to the schedule even when circumstances have changed, and you end up neglecting more important responsibilities.

Chaos: any minor change ruins your whole day, and then you abandon all your priorities.

Look for a better way: a clear but flexible schedule.

Discipline doesn’t mean never departing from the plan. It also means knowing how to return to it.

11. Build your own day, don’t copy someone else’s

On the internet you will find people who wake up at four in the morning, read for an hour, run, take a cold shower, write in a journal, work ten hours, and end the day with an impeccable routine.

Some habits can be useful. But the fact that a schedule works for someone else doesn’t mean it is right for you.

Maybe that person has a different schedule, different responsibilities, a different energy level, or different conditions.

You don’t have to build your life in order to reproduce the routine of someone you follow online.

You have to identify the good principles and adapt them.

If you get up early but sleep too little, you haven’t discovered the secret of success. You have moved your tiredness to an earlier hour.

If you spend the whole day working and no longer have time for family, rest, or personal responsibilities, your productivity may have too high a cost.

If you schedule three hours of Bible study, but do it out of comparison and end up considering yourself superior to those who have a different rhythm, discipline has lost part of its purpose.

Don’t build a schedule that looks impressive. Build one that helps you live responsibly before God and toward people.

12. Practical exercise: design the next 24 hours

Now is the time to apply this.

You don’t have to reorganize your whole life in a single evening. Begin by planning the next day.

My plan for tomorrow

Fill it out in a few minutes. A short plan that you follow is more useful than a perfect one you don’t use.

After you fill it out, check whether the schedule is realistic.

Have you left time for fixed responsibilities? Have you included breaks? Have you reserved enough time for sleep? Have you decided when you will do the main task, not just that you will do it sometime?

You don’t have to plan every minute. A few clear markers are enough.

13. A seven-day plan for a more organized life

Over the next week, try changing one thing at a time.

The seven-day challenge

Don’t evaluate the week only by the number of boxes checked.

Ask yourself whether you have become more aware of how you use your time, whether you have reduced needless interruptions, and whether you have managed to give more attention to the important things.

If a rule didn’t work, don’t immediately assume you have no discipline. Maybe the rule was too ambitious, maybe the environment didn’t help you, or maybe you need a different strategy.

Analyze and adjust.

14. Questions for reflection

  • Who or what most often sets my priorities: my values, my responsibilities, or my notifications?
  • What are the most important things I put off because they don’t seem urgent?
  • At what times of day am I most vulnerable to distractions?
  • Am I building my schedule around real responsibilities or copying the routines of other people?
  • What digital activity consumes my time without giving me a proportional benefit?
  • Do I leave enough time for rest and for the people I love?
  • What is one single change I can begin tomorrow and realistically maintain?

Don’t try to change ten habits at once. Choose a change important enough to be worth the effort and realistic enough that you can keep it up.

Chapter 8

How to Work Up Gradually to Two or Three Hours of Scripture and Prayer

There is a difference between wanting a deep life with God and organizing your life so that you cultivate that relationship.

Many Christians want to pray more, read the Bible attentively, and know Christ more deeply. They resolve to get up earlier, read more chapters, and spend time in prayer. In the first days they are motivated. Then tiredness sets in, their schedule fills up, they wake up late, and they abandon the plan.

The problem is not always a lack of love for God. Sometimes the problem is that they are trying to build in a week a habit they haven’t cultivated for months or years.

If you want to reach two or three hours a day of prayer and Bible study, you don’t need only motivation. You need a realistic strategy, perseverance, and a healthy understanding of spiritual discipline.

But first of all, we have to establish an essential truth.

Two or three hours is not a universal biblical standard by which a person’s spirituality is measured. You can have an impressive schedule and an inattentive heart. You can spend less time in the Word during a certain period because of real responsibilities, without that automatically meaning you love God less.

The goal is not to accumulate hours. The goal is to grow in the knowledge of God, in obedience, in love, and in likeness to Christ.

Hours are a tool. Your relationship with God and the fruit of a life lived with Him are the goal.

1. Understand why you need spiritual discipline

In John 15:4–5, Jesus uses the image of the vine and the branches to explain the disciples’ dependence on Him. A branch cannot bear fruit by itself unless it remains in the vine.

The principle is profound: the Christian life is not based solely on human ambition, but on remaining in Christ.

Then why do we still need discipline?

Because the relationship with God has to be cultivated intentionally. Not to convince Him to love us, but to orient our lives toward Him.

Think about a few differences:

  • You don’t pray to buy God’s love, but because you need Him.
  • You don’t read the Bible to check off an obligation, but to know the truth and learn to live in obedience.
  • You don’t fast, pray, and study Scripture to prove that you are superior to other Christians.
  • You don’t pursue an impressive spiritual schedule, but a life in which your faith is also seen in ordinary choices.

Ephesians 2:8–10 holds two truths together: we are saved by grace, through faith, not by our own works; at the same time, we are called to walk in the good works God prepared beforehand.

So spiritual discipline is not the price of salvation. It is one of the ways you organize your life in order to grow and respond with obedience to the grace you have received.

Don’t turn discipline into a means of proving to God that you deserve to be His. Use discipline to constantly orient your life toward the One to whom you belong by faith.

2. Don’t start with three hours. Start with the truth about yourself.

Imagine that right now you pray ten minutes a day and read a chapter of the Bible when you remember.

One evening, you hear the testimony of a Christian who says he spends three hours a day in prayer and Scripture. You feel challenged and resolve to do the same starting tomorrow.

You get up early, try to pray for an hour, read many chapters, and fill your notebook with notes. In the first days you manage. Then comes a morning when you are tired. On another day you have exams or family responsibilities. In the end, you lose the rhythm and end up doing almost nothing.

What happened?

You set a target without building the capacity to sustain it.

Proverbs 21:5 speaks about the value of the plans of the diligent, in contrast to the haste that leads to lack. The principle can be applied here too: lasting growth requires planning, not just enthusiasm.

Before you decide how much time you will spend with God, answer three questions honestly:

  • How much time do I actually spend now in prayer and in attentive reading of Scripture?
  • What most often prevents me from keeping a schedule: my phone, insufficient sleep, lack of a plan, tiredness, or procrastination?
  • What is the next step small enough that I can repeat it even on an ordinary day?

Don’t answer according to the person you would like to be. Answer according to how you live today.

The truth about your starting point is not a condemnation. It is the information you need in order to build something real.

3. Build gradually, not through an explosion of motivation

A program of prayer and Bible study can grow progressively. The exact pace depends on your schedule, your responsibilities, and how you adapt.

That doesn’t mean you have failed.

It means you are building a practice you can keep even when life doesn’t look like a motivational video.

4. You don’t have to do everything in a single session

One of the most common obstacles is the idea that valuable spiritual time must necessarily be uninterrupted and very long.

There is value in spending an extended period in prayer. But you don’t have to compress the whole schedule into a single session, especially if you are just starting out.

Look at a few biblical examples:

  • In Mark 1:35, Jesus withdraws early in the morning for prayer.
  • In Luke 5:16, we read that Jesus withdrew to lonely places and prayed.
  • In Matthew 14:23, after sending the crowds away, Jesus goes up on the mountain to pray.

These passages show that prayer had an intentional place in His life. They do not, however, give us a universal number of hours that every Christian must reproduce.

You can organize your day into three main moments:

Morning: orienting the heart

20–45 minutes

Read a passage, notice what it says, pray, and decide which truth you want to keep in mind during the day. Don’t automatically start with notifications.

Midday: returning to God

10–30 minutes

Stop, thank God, bring your worries before Him, and re-read a verse or an idea from that morning.

Evening: going deeper and examining

20–60 minutes

Study a text more carefully, write down questions, research the context, and end the day with prayer, thanksgiving, and a sincere confession of the things that need to be set right.

These are examples, not obligations. If you have classes, a job, a family, or other responsibilities, adapt the schedule. You can have two sessions instead of three, or you can start with ten minutes in the morning and ten in the evening.

The goal is to integrate closeness to God into real life, not to build an imaginary life in which no responsibility exists.

5. Learn to pray, not just to spend time trying to pray

You can sit for an hour with your eyes closed and discover that your mind has run off in every direction. That doesn’t automatically mean your prayer was useless. It means you need to learn to bring your attention back.

In Matthew 6:5–13, Jesus warns the disciples against prayer done to be seen by people and against heaping up empty words, and then gives them a model of prayer. The problem is not the length of the prayer itself, but the attitude of the heart and how we relate to God.

You can use the Lord’s Prayer as a guiding structure.

A simple structure for prayer

1. Worship

Direct your attention to who God is, to His character, and to what Scripture reveals about Him.

2. Thanksgiving

Name concrete things you are grateful for. Thanksgiving helps you look beyond the list of your problems.

3. Confession and examination

Acknowledge sin sincerely, along with wrong motives and the areas where you need change. Don’t confuse repentance with hating yourself.

4. Intercession

Pray for your family, your church, friends, leaders, people who are suffering, and those who don’t yet know Christ.

5. Surrender and obedience

Ask for help to carry out what you have understood from Scripture. Think of a concrete step of obedience, not just an emotion you would like to feel.

You don’t have to spend exactly the same number of minutes on each category. Sometimes you will have an urgent need; at other times you will spend more time interceding for someone.

And when you don’t know how to pray, you can come before God with sincerity. Romans 8:26 speaks about the Spirit’s help in our weakness. You don’t need to invent impressive words in order to seem spiritual.

God doesn’t need a sophisticated verbal presentation in order to know the truth of your heart.

6. Read the Bible to understand it, not just to finish the plan

Reading ten chapters without following their meaning is not necessarily more valuable than carefully studying a shorter passage.

There is a difference between reading and study.

Reading helps you see the big picture of Scripture. Study helps you understand a text more carefully, in its context. Both are useful and they complement each other.

For a study session, you can use the following questions:

  • Observation: What does the text actually say?
  • Context: Who is speaking, to whom, in what situation, and what happens before and after?
  • Meaning: What did the message mean to the first recipients?
  • Connection with the rest of Scripture: How does this passage fit with the broader biblical teaching?
  • Application: What should I believe, correct, keep, or do in light of the text?

Let’s take as an example James 1:22, which calls its readers to be doers of the Word, not just hearers.

You can read the verse, underline it, and write it in a notebook. But the decisive question is: how does obedience to the Word show up today in my behavior?

Maybe you need to ask forgiveness. Maybe you need to stop sharing content that humiliates others. Maybe you need to be more honest, carry out your responsibilities, or help someone without waiting for recognition.

Application must flow from the meaning of the text, not from an idea invented merely because it sounds spiritual.

And if you come across a difficult passage, don’t hurry to build a doctrine on the basis of a single expression. Read the context, compare the relevant passages, and use serious study resources.

You don’t have to understand every verse perfectly for the time spent in Scripture to have value.

7. What do you do when your mind wanders during prayer?

You begin to pray. After two minutes, you are thinking about an exam. Then about a conversation from yesterday. Then you remember you were supposed to reply to someone. In the end, you realize you have spent a few minutes thinking about everything except what you were doing.

Don’t turn this experience into a conclusion about your spiritual worth.

Attention can be trained, and concentration can be affected by tiredness, stress, your surroundings, and the habit of constantly checking your phone.

When you notice your mind wandering, try the following:

  • Return without drama. Don’t spend the next five minutes condemning yourself for the two minutes of inattention.
  • Write the thought down. If you remember an important task, write it on a sheet of paper and return to prayer.
  • Use Scripture. Slowly read a psalm or a passage and respond to God in prayer, starting from what you have read.
  • Reduce stimuli. Put your phone aside, turn off notifications, and choose a place where you can have quiet.
  • Shorten the session if necessary. It is better to build a realistic habit than to mechanically prolong a session in which you are exhausted.

Psalm 62:8 urges believers to pour out their hearts before God. Prayer doesn’t require you to pretend you have no worries. You can tell God exactly what concerns you and ask for His help.

Not every distraction disappears instantly. Sometimes you have to return to prayer several times. The returning is part of the practice.

8. How to avoid turning the three hours into an idol

Here is a subtle danger.

You can begin by wanting to know God more and end up preoccupied mostly with your own spiritual performance.

You begin to count the minutes. You compare your schedule with others’. You feel superior when you have managed three hours and worthless when you have managed only thirty minutes.

Instead of helping you grow, discipline becomes a source of pride or condemnation.

Jesus warns in Matthew 6 against religious practices done to receive the approval of people. The principle also applies when the audience is imaginary: you can end up organizing your life to maintain the image of the very spiritual person you want to see in the mirror.

Ask yourself honestly:

  • Am I drawing near to God or trying to prove I am better than others?
  • Does the way I read Scripture make me more willing to listen?
  • Does my discipline help me love people, or do I become impatient and contemptuous toward those who have a different pace?
  • Am I carrying out my responsibilities, or am I using the spiritual schedule to run from them?
  • Can I thank God for the time spent with Him without comparing myself to anyone else?

If you had a poor day, you don’t have to give up everything. If you had a very good day, you don’t have to consider yourself superior.

You are not the sum of your spiritual hours. You are a person called to walk with God in faith, repentance, and obedience.

10. When you fail, return to God, not to self-condemnation

There will be days when you won’t keep the plan.

Maybe you slept poorly. Maybe you had a busy day. Maybe you chose to waste time on your phone, even though you knew what you had resolved. You don’t have to call every wrong choice a simple accident, but neither should you turn a bad day into a verdict on your whole spiritual life.

In 1 John 1:9, the confession of sins is tied to the faithfulness and justice of God, who forgives and cleanses. The Christian response to sin is not hiding, justifying oneself, or despair, but a sincere return to God.

Then you go back to the next step.

You don’t have to make up for all the lost minutes. You don’t have to double tomorrow’s schedule to compensate for today. You don’t have to wait for the beginning of a new week to start over.

You can open the Bible now. You can pray now. You can put your phone aside now.

Sometimes the most important step of the day is not adding another thirty minutes, but dropping an excuse and doing what you already know has to be done.

And remember: God is not a stopwatch waiting to catch you a minute short of what you resolved.

Chapter 9

What to Do When You Don’t Feel Like It, You’re Tired, or You’ve Lost a Day

There is a picture of the spiritual life that we sometimes build in our minds: you get up early, open the Bible, pray with passion, understand every passage, organize your day perfectly, and stay focused on the things of God until evening.

Then real life arrives.

You wake up tired. You slept too little. You have classes, exams, work, or personal problems. You try to pray, but your mind wanders. You put off reading the Bible until evening, and in the evening you are too tired to do what you set out to do.

One day you don’t manage. The next day you try again, but you don’t have the same motivation. After a few days, the thought appears: “I’ve ruined everything. I’ll start over on Monday.”

And so a lost day turns into a week, and a week into a habit.

Spiritual maturity doesn’t mean you will never have difficult days. It means you learn to remain faithful to God and to return to what is good even when the initial enthusiasm disappears.

This chapter is not about how to force yourself to function regardless of your condition. It is about how to tell a lack of motivation from real tiredness, neglect from human limits, and repentance from needless condemnation.

1. Don’t confuse motivation with faithfulness

Motivation is changeable.

Sometimes you read a biblical passage and feel that every verse is speaking directly to you. At other times you read the same passage and have the impression that your mind is somewhere else. Sometimes you pray with ease. At other times you have to choose deliberately to stop the agitation and draw near to God.

If you build your whole spiritual life on what you feel each day, you will have an unstable schedule.

In Luke 9:23, Jesus speaks about denying oneself and taking up the cross daily for the one who wants to come after Him. The context is the call to discipleship, not a productivity technique. Still, the expression “daily” reminds us that following Jesus is not just an emotional decision made at a special moment, but a continuous orientation of life.

Sometimes obedience begins with a strong feeling. At other times it begins with a quiet decision.

You get out of bed and pray, even though you don’t feel the same intensity as yesterday. You open the Bible and read a passage attentively, even if you have no spectacular revelation. You carry out your responsibilities, even if you receive no applause.

But note: this doesn’t mean you must ignore your emotions or force yourself to the point of exhaustion. It means not turning them into the only criterion by which you choose what is good.

Not everything good feels easy, and not everything difficult is automatically God’s will. You need discernment, Scripture, and wisdom.

Exercise: identify the real reason

The next time you put off prayer or Bible reading, stop and ask yourself:

  • Am I truly exhausted, or do I just not feel like starting?
  • Is it hard for me to concentrate because I am worried?
  • Did I sleep enough?
  • Have I already spent a lot of time on my phone and accustomed my mind to rapid stimulation?
  • Am I trying to do too many things in a single day?
  • Am I avoiding a biblical truth because I know I will have to change my behavior?

The answers may differ from one day to the next. That is exactly why you need honesty, not a single explanation for all situations.

2. What do you do when you don’t feel like it?

Suppose you have decided to read the Bible in the morning for twenty minutes. The alarm goes off. You wake up and your first thought is: “I don’t feel like it. I’ll stay a little longer.”

You don’t have to start by negotiating with yourself for fifteen minutes. You need a concrete step.

The small-start rule

Instead of telling yourself that you have to have a perfect twenty-minute session, resolve to start with five minutes of real attention.

  • Get up and prepare the place.
  • Put your phone aside.
  • Read a short passage, without hurrying.
  • Notice what the text says.
  • Pray sincerely about what you have read.

After you have begun, you can continue up to the set length. If on that day you truly need a shorter session, don’t turn the exception into a reason for condemnation.

Why does this approach work? Because sometimes the main obstacle is not the activity itself, but the resistance at the beginning.

Proverbs 13:4 contrasts the desire of the lazy person with the diligence of the hard worker. The principle is that desire alone does not produce the intended result. There must also be action.

The spiritual application is simple: you can want a prayer life very much, but desire has to be turned into concrete choices.

Don’t always wait for motivation to appear. Sometimes you begin with a small step, and concentration forms along the way.

Still, don’t turn this idea into a new rigid rule. If you are sick, very tired, or going through a difficult period, maybe you need rest and support, not one more method by which to pressure yourself.

3. What do you do when you’re tired?

Here we must make an important distinction.

There is a difference between the tiredness you feel because it is hard to start an activity and real exhaustion that affects your ability to function.

The first may require discipline. The second may require rest, an adjustment of your schedule, or help.

In Mark 6:31, Jesus tells the disciples to come away to a quiet place and rest a while. The context is one in which their activity was intense and people were coming and going constantly. Rest appears here as a real human need, not as an enemy of the spiritual life.

You don’t need less wisdom just because you have big spiritual goals.

If you constantly sleep too little in order to gain more prayer time, you risk affecting your health, concentration, and responsibilities. A spiritual schedule shouldn’t be built by repeatedly sacrificing the sleep you need.

Three questions for tiring days

1. Why am I tired?

Is it a night of insufficient sleep, a demanding day, or a disorganized schedule? Or am I using tiredness as a justification, even though I have energy for hours of phone use?

2. What can I realistically do today?

Maybe instead of an hour of Bible study you can read one passage, pray for a few minutes, and return to a normal schedule after you rest.

3. What do I need to change for tomorrow?

If you are always too tired for the things you consider important, the problem may not be a lack of willpower. Maybe you need to go to bed earlier, reduce your time online, or reorganize your responsibilities.

Not all problems are solved by more effort. Some are solved by better organization.

4. What do you do when you’ve lost a day?

You had a plan. You didn’t keep it. Maybe you spent hours on your phone, put off the important things, and reached evening without having done what you set out to do.

Now you have two possibilities.

The first is to acknowledge what happened, understand the cause, and resume the plan.

The second is to condemn yourself, tell yourself you are not disciplined enough, and put off returning until you are in a good state again.

The second option sometimes seems more serious, but it repairs nothing.

In 1 John 1:9, the apostle John speaks about confessing sins and about God’s faithfulness to forgive and cleanse. The verse is not a formula by which every organizational failure automatically becomes sin. If you consciously chose to neglect your responsibilities, you need honesty and, where appropriate, repentance. If you were prevented by a legitimate situation, you need discernment, not artificial guilt.

In both situations, you can take the next step.

You don’t have to wait for a new week to resume a good habit.

If you lost the morning, you can read in the evening. If you lost the day, you can begin again tomorrow. If you abandoned the schedule for a week, you can return today with a realistic goal.

Don’t try to mechanically make up all the lost hours. The goal is not to pay off a debt of minutes, but to reorient your life.

The return protocol

Step 1. Acknowledge reality

Say concretely what happened. “I spent two hours on my phone instead of doing my plan” is more useful than “I’m an incapable person.”

Step 2. Identify the trigger

Were you tired, did you open an app out of reflex, did you put off a difficult task, or had you not set a clear schedule?

Step 3. Return to the next step

Read the planned passage or pray for a few minutes. You don’t have to turn the return into a marathon session of compensation.

Step 4. Change the system

Put your phone in another room, change the time of the session, or temporarily shrink the goal. Don’t rely on the promise that tomorrow you will suddenly have more willpower.

5. Beware the trap of spiritual perfectionism

Perfectionism can sometimes wear religious clothes.

You resolve to read five chapters. You read only two and consider the day a failure. You resolve to pray for an hour. You prayed for forty minutes, but instead of thanking God for the time spent, you focus exclusively on the twenty missing minutes.

Over time, you can end up measuring every spiritual experience by the distance between reality and the ideal plan.

But discipline is not the same as perfectionism.

Discipline helps you return to what matters. Perfectionism can make you give up because you didn’t manage to reach a rigid standard.

In Philippians 3:12–14, Paul speaks about the fact that he has not already reached perfection, but continues to run toward the goal. The passage is about his progress in the calling he received from Christ, not about a productivity technique. Still, it shows us an important attitude: acknowledging that we are still in process does not oblige us to stand still.

Growth involves seeing what needs to change and continuing to move forward.

Instead of asking only “Did I do everything I set out to do?”, ask also:

  • Was I sincere before God?
  • Did I understand something from Scripture and apply that truth?
  • Did I identify a habit that keeps me from growing?
  • What can I do more wisely tomorrow?

Don’t lower your standards just to feel good. But don’t invent standards that God has not imposed either.

6. When you feel nothing in prayer

Sometimes the problem is not that you don’t have time or don’t feel like it. The problem is that you pray and don’t feel what you expected to feel.

Maybe you got used to intense moments of prayer and now everything seems quieter. Maybe you are going through a period of worry, sadness, or confusion. Maybe you read Scripture but don’t have the impression that you understand anything new.

Don’t immediately conclude that God has abandoned you or that you have become a weaker Christian.

Feelings are real, but they are not the only measure of spiritual reality. The absence of a strong emotion does not, by itself, prove the absence of God’s work.

In Psalm 13, the psalmist addresses painful questions to God about feeling forgotten. The psalm does not hide the anxiety, but brings it before God and ends by expressing trust in His goodness.

This is an important lesson: you can come to God even when you don’t have an impressive emotional state.

You can say:

“Lord, I am tired and I don’t know exactly what I feel. Help me understand Your Word, be sincere before You, and remain faithful in what I already know.”

You don’t have to manufacture emotions in order to have a sincere prayer. Nor should you assume that every difficult period is a sign of a hidden spiritual problem.

If a lack of energy, sadness, or difficulty concentrating persists and affects your daily life, it may be helpful to talk with a trusted person or a specialist as well. Faith and responsible care for your health are not incompatible.

7. Don’t confuse rest with carelessness

There are two opposite mistakes.

The first is to use rest as an excuse to constantly avoid important things.

The second is to consider any rest a proof of spiritual weakness.

Neither is wise.

Jesus did not live out His ministry as if human needs did not exist. The Gospels show Him praying, serving, going to secluded places, and taking part in people’s lives. We shouldn’t build from these accounts a rigid schedule for every Christian, but they keep us from reducing the spiritual life to a permanent race of performance.

Sometimes the wisest choice is to reduce the time spent on your phone and use it for prayer. At other times, it is to go to bed on time so that you can carry out your responsibilities the next day.

At other times, you need to go for a walk, talk with someone, or resolve a problem you have kept putting off.

Rest must not be used to run from God, and spiritual discipline must not be used to run from the responsibilities of life.

8. Build a plan for difficult days

So far, you have worked on the ideal schedule. But you also need a plan for the days when things don’t go as expected.

This can be your minimum plan.

Minimum plan for a difficult day

It is not a permanent substitute for your goals. It is a realistic version that helps you keep your connection with important habits when your schedule is crowded.

This plan must not become an excuse to stay permanently at the minimum. If you have decided you want to grow, keep working on your usual schedule.

But don’t despise a small step taken sincerely on a difficult day, either.

In Luke 16:10, Jesus speaks about faithfulness in little things. The immediate context is the use of what has been entrusted, but the principle of responsibility in small things can also be applied to how we form habits.

Sometimes character shows precisely in how you return to what matters after you have lost the rhythm.

9. How to turn a failure into a lesson

At the end of a difficult day, don’t limit yourself to saying “tomorrow will be better.”

This promise sounds good, but it doesn’t change anything automatically.

Instead, write down the answers to four questions:

  • What happened? Describe the situation concretely, without exaggerating.
  • What contributed to it? Maybe you slept too little, started the day on your phone, or set unrealistic goals.
  • What was in my control? Separate your own choices from circumstances you couldn’t control.
  • What will I change tomorrow? Choose a single concrete modification.

Example:

“I didn’t read the Bible in the morning because I hit snooze and then checked my phone. Tomorrow I will put my phone far from the bed and set the Bible out on my desk the night before.”

Notice the difference between this approach and the statement “I’m not spiritual enough.”

The first identifies a behavior and proposes a solution. The second turns an incident into a general judgment about oneself.

If the problem is a habit, work on the habit. If it is a wrong choice, acknowledge the choice. If it is a real limit, adjust the plan. If it is a persistent problem that overwhelms you, seek support.

That is how you learn from experience instead of repeating the same cycle of enthusiasm, failure, and abandonment.

Questions for reflection

  • What do I usually do when I don’t feel like praying or reading the Bible?
  • Do I sometimes use guilt as a substitute for concrete change?
  • What is the difference, in my life, between a lack of motivation and real tiredness?
  • What happens after I lose a day: do I return to the plan or abandon it for a while?
  • What practical change could most reduce the risk of repeating the same mistake?
  • Have I come to measure myself by the length of my prayer or by the impression I think I make on others?
  • How can I stay consistent without ignoring the need for rest and my responsibilities toward others?

Chapter 10

You Cannot Live Out Discipleship in Isolation

There is a subtle danger in the desire to become a disciplined Christian: you can become so preoccupied with your own spiritual growth that you forget the people God is calling you to show love to.

You read the Bible. You pray. You organize your time. You try to give up distractions and form healthy habits. All of these can be good things.

But what happens when someone needs your help and you are too busy with your personal schedule? How do you react when a coworker irritates you? What do you do when someone asks for your time even though you had planned to study? How do you treat your family when you are tired?

You can know many verses about love and still be impatient with people. You can spend a lot of time in prayer and avoid being reconciled with someone. You can listen to sermons about humility and consider yourself superior to those who don’t have your discipline.

That is why you need to understand a fundamental truth:

The purpose of spiritual discipline is not to make you a person exclusively preoccupied with your own spirituality, but a disciple of Jesus Christ who loves God and loves his neighbor.

Personal faith is essential. Personal prayer is important. Individual study of Scripture has a real place in the Christian life. But none of these replaces community, love, and service.

1. Jesus did not call you only to a private relationship with Him

In Mark 3:13–14, Jesus calls to Himself those He wants and appoints the twelve, among other things, to be with Him and to send them out to preach.

Notice the two dimensions: the disciples are called to be with Jesus and they are sent out into a work.

First, the relationship with Him. Then, being sent into the world. They are not two directions that exclude each other, but two aspects of their calling.

The same principle helps us understand the Christian life. You need time to stand before God, listen to His Word, and pray. But you must not turn that time into a permanent refuge from the people you are called to love.

In John 13:34–35, Jesus gives the disciples the command to love one another and says that their love for each other will be a sign by which others will recognize them as His disciples.

He does not say that they will be recognized only by how much they know, how much they pray, or how well they organize their schedule.

Knowing the truth matters. Prayer matters. Obedience matters. But love is not a decorative element alongside these.

You cannot separate spiritual maturity from how you treat people.

If your discipline helps you be more patient, more honest, more responsible, and more willing to serve, it is producing good fruit. If it makes you permanently irritated, contemptuous, and preoccupied with proving that you are more spiritual than others, it is time to examine your motives.

2. Don’t use your time with God to run from people

Sometimes withdrawal is necessary. Jesus Himself withdrew for prayer, as we see in Luke 5:16.

You need quiet. You need moments when you are not available for every notification and every request. You need to protect your attention and not spend the whole day reacting to what others want from you.

But there is a difference between a withdrawal that helps you draw near to God and an isolation by which you constantly avoid relational responsibilities.

Suppose you had a conflict with a coworker. Instead of talking honestly with him, you begin to fill your schedule with spiritual activities. You read more, pray more, and listen to sermons about character. Yet you continue to avoid the necessary conversation.

Your schedule may look very good, but the relational problem remains unresolved.

In Matthew 5:23–24, Jesus speaks about the importance of being reconciled with your brother before continuing the act of worship described in the passage. The context is specific, but the principle is clear: the relationship with God must not be used to ignore the wrong we have done or the responsibility of seeking reconciliation.

This doesn’t mean that every conflict is resolved immediately or that reconciliation depends only on you. Sometimes the other person refuses dialogue. At other times, you need healthy boundaries, support, or time to process what happened.

But don’t use spiritual language to justify running from responsibility.

Ask yourself:

  • Is there someone I need to ask forgiveness from?
  • Have I put off an important conversation out of pride?
  • Am I hiding in religious activities to avoid a problem?
  • Have I become so preoccupied with my schedule that I no longer notice the needs of those close to me?

Sometimes the next step of obedience is not to read another chapter. It is to put into practice the truth you have already read.

3. The church is not a performance hall for spiritual consumers

We live in a time when you can listen to sermons from morning to night, follow Christian conferences, read books, and have access to an enormous amount of biblical content without leaving your room.

This access can be helpful. You can learn from competent people, study topics you wouldn’t have discovered on your own, and find explanations for difficult passages.

But there is also a risk: confusing the consumption of Christian content with participation in Christian life.

You can watch a sermon about service without serving anyone. You can listen to a series about prayer without praying. You can watch a testimony about generosity without being willing to help a person in need.

In Acts 2:42–47, the life of the first believers is described through devotion to the apostles’ teaching, fellowship, the breaking of bread, and prayer, alongside a concrete community life.

It was not just a collection of individuals following religious content separately. Faith was expressed through worship, relationships, mutual help, and a shared life.

In 1 Corinthians 12, Paul uses the image of one body made up of many members to explain the diversity and interdependence of believers. Each member has a role, and none can claim that it doesn’t need the others.

This means you are not called only to receive. You are also called to contribute.

Maybe you don’t have a visible position. Maybe you don’t preach, sing on stage, or lead a group. But you can listen to a person going through a difficult time, help with a practical activity, encourage a coworker, or be someone people can trust.

You don’t have to be in front of a crowd in order to serve.

Faith doesn’t become more real because it is more visible.

4. Choose community, not just an audience

There is a difference between having many people who follow your content and having relationships in which people really know you.

You can receive appreciation for a Christian post without anyone knowing whether you are struggling with discouragement. You can share verses about faith without having a person you can tell honestly when things are hard. You can attend many events without building stable relationships.

Christian community doesn’t mean only being present in the same room. It means learning to live alongside other people, with their differences, weaknesses, gifts, and responsibilities.

In Hebrews 10:24–25, believers are urged to stir one another up to love and good works and not to give up meeting together.

Notice the purpose: closeness between believers should help them grow in love and good works.

It is not only about being present at a meeting. It is about a life in which you encourage others and let others encourage you.

Three things you can do

1. Build at least a few sincere relationships.

Look for people with whom you can talk openly about faith, questions, difficulties, and responsibilities. You don’t need dozens of close friends, but real relationships.

2. Learn to receive correction.

Proverbs 27:17 uses the image of iron sharpening iron to illustrate the mutual influence between people. Sometimes a brother or sister in Christ can notice a sore spot you don’t see.

Listen with humility, but keep your discernment. Not every criticism is correct, and not every person is right just because he speaks in religious language.

3. Be present for others too.

Don’t look for community only when you need encouragement. Learn to listen, to ask, to help, and to be consistent in your relationships.

Community is not a service you consume. It is a responsibility you share with others.

5. How to love people without losing your discipline

Maybe you are wondering: if I have to be available for people, how can I still protect my time for prayer and Bible study?

The question is legitimate.

You are not obliged to respond instantly to every message. You don’t have to accept every activity. You can’t solve all the problems of all people. And it isn’t healthy to abandon your responsibilities every time a request arises.

Love doesn’t mean the absence of boundaries. It means using boundaries wisely and not turning them into walls by which you avoid any inconvenience.

You can set a time for prayer and study. You can politely explain that you aren’t available at that moment. You can answer a message later. At the same time, if an urgent need arises, you can reevaluate the plan.

Mature discipline is not the rigidity that refuses any change. It is the ability to stay oriented toward what matters, including when you have to adapt.

An example of a balanced schedule

AreaConcrete practice
Relationship with GodKeep a realistic time for prayer and Scripture.
Christian communityTake part consistently in the life of the church and get involved in real relationships.
Family and friendsGive attention without being constantly distracted by your phone.
Daily responsibilitiesCarry out your tasks with seriousness and integrity.
ServiceChoose a concrete form of help that you can sustain.
RestKeep time for recovery, without confusing it with permanent avoidance of responsibilities.

You don’t have to excel in every area at the same time, every day. But it is important not to use one good area to systematically neglect another.

6. Love is seen in little things

In Luke 10:25–37, Jesus tells the parable of the good Samaritan in the context of the question about who is my neighbor. The story contrasts people who pass by a wounded man with one who stops, gives him help, and takes on a cost for his good.

It is not just a lesson about good feelings. The Samaritan does something concrete.

He interrupts his journey. He draws near. He provides care. He arranges the help that is needed.

The application for your life doesn’t have to be spectacular.

Maybe your classmate is overwhelmed by exams. Maybe someone at church is going through a difficult time. Maybe a family member needs to be listened to without your checking your phone every two minutes.

Maybe there is a practical activity you can help with, without receiving recognition.

These situations are not needless interruptions of the spiritual life. They can become places where you live out what you have learned.

Of course, not every request has to be accepted and not every problem is yours. The Samaritan offers an example of concrete compassion, not a command to take on all the needs of the world alone.

The question is whether you are willing to see the person in front of you and respond with wisdom.

Don’t measure your whole spiritual growth by what you do when you are alone. Look also at how you behave when someone interrupts your plan, contradicts you, or needs help.

7. Don’t try to be everyone’s savior

There is also a trap opposite to isolation: believing that if you want to love people, you must be available to every person and solve every problem.

You can’t do that.

You have limits. You have responsibilities. You need rest. Some situations call for specialized help, and others exceed what you can do alone.

Galatians 6:2 urges believers to bear one another’s burdens, and a few verses later, in Galatians 6:5, Paul also speaks about personal responsibility. These statements do not contradict each other: there are burdens we carry together and responsibilities that each person must take on for himself.

You can support a friend without becoming responsible for all his choices. You can listen to someone without promising to fix the situation. You can encourage a person to seek appropriate help when the problem exceeds your abilities.

Mature love does not mean consuming yourself until you can’t go on. It means being available responsibly.

Nor should you feel guilty every time you say “no.” The important thing is not to use boundaries as a pretext for indifference, nor to confuse sacrifice with a total lack of discernment.

8. Turn what you learn into concrete service

So far in this book, you have worked on attention, the phone, procrastination, discipline, prayer, and reading Scripture.

Now you have to ask yourself: what is changing in the lives of the people around me because of how I choose to live?

Not in the sense that you must control the results or take credit for other people’s changes. But in the sense that Christian love must become visible in your choices.

If you have learned about patience, practice patience when you are contradicted.

If you have studied forgiveness, examine whether there is a person toward whom you are holding resentment.

If you have prayed for people in need, look for a wise way to help them.

If you have learned about truth, be honest even when a lie would bring you an advantage.

If you have understood the importance of humility, accept that you can be wrong and that you need to learn from others.

In James 1:22, readers are urged to be doers of the Word, not just hearers. The application is direct: biblical knowledge must not remain merely accumulated information.

You can have a notebook full of notes and still put off obeying a truth you already know.

That is why, after each study session, add one question:

“What is one concrete thing I can put into practice in the next 24 hours?”

You don’t have to invent an action for every verse. But when the text confronts you with a clear responsibility, don’t be satisfied with only underlining it.

9. A practical exercise for the next seven days

Over the next week, keep your time for prayer and Scripture, but add an intentional relational dimension.

The challenge: a faith that can be seen

Check off each practice after you have done it. You don’t have to do anything spectacular. Aim for consistency and sincerity.

Don’t use the challenge to build an image of yourself as a good person. Use it to notice where you can grow and which habits would help you love better.

Sometimes you will see results immediately. At other times, relationships will remain complicated. You can’t control the other person’s response, but you can work on your own sincerity, patience, and responsibility.

Questions for reflection

  • Have I ever turned the spiritual life into an individual project in which others seem to be merely obstacles?
  • When someone interrupts my plan, do I react with love or with irritation?
  • Am I involved in a Christian community where I can both receive and contribute?
  • Is there a relationship I am avoiding out of pride or fear?
  • What biblical truth do I know but need to put into practice more clearly?
  • How can I serve without seeking the approval of people and without neglecting my responsibilities?
  • What healthy boundary do I need to set in order to love people consistently?

Chapter 11

From Spiritual Accumulation to Obedience and Service

It is not enough to know what God says. The goal is for His truth to transform how you live.

There is a subtle danger in the Christian life: you can come to know a great deal about God without that knowledge significantly changing how you treat people, use your time, or carry out your responsibilities.

You can listen to sermons every day, underline verses in your Bible, read Christian books, and have complex theological conversations. All of these can be helpful. The problem arises when the accumulation of information becomes a substitute for obedience.

You can come to know what the Bible says about forgiveness and still refuse to forgive. You can know verses about humility and never accept being corrected. You can listen to sermons about love and treat with contempt the person who interrupts you just as you begin your prayer time.

It isn’t necessarily information that is missing. Sometimes what is missing is the step between the truth heard and the truth lived.

This chapter is not an exhortation to do more things in order to prove to God that you deserve His love. It is a call to respond with obedience to the love you have already received in Christ.

1. Knowledge that produces no change risks becoming an illusion

Read James 1:22–25.

James urges believers to be doers of the Word, not just hearers who deceive themselves.

Notice the wording: the problem is not hearing the Word. Hearing is necessary. The problem arises when a person confuses the fact that he has heard the truth with the fact that he has responded to the truth.

Imagine looking in a mirror and noticing that you have something on your face. You walk away from the mirror without doing anything and behave as if the mere fact that you saw the problem had solved it.

It is absurd, but exactly this mechanism can creep into the spiritual life.

  • You read about prayer, but you keep putting it off without changing anything.
  • You read about controlling the tongue, but you keep speaking impulsively.
  • You read about generosity, but you aren’t willing to help anyone.
  • You read about forgiveness, but you nurse resentments.
  • You read about purity, but you deliberately keep habits that take you away from your values.

Not all change happens instantly. Some struggles take time, maturing, support, and many returnings to God. But there must be a difference between the person who is sincerely struggling to obey and the person who uses spiritual accumulation to avoid obedience.

The question is not only “What did I learn today?”, but also “How should I respond to what I learned?”

2. Obedience doesn’t buy God’s grace

Before we go further, we need to establish an essential distinction.

Ephesians 2:8–10 shows that salvation is by grace, through faith, not the result of our works, and verse 10 speaks about the good works for which we were created in Christ Jesus.

The order matters.

You don’t do good works to oblige God to love you. You don’t serve at church to buy your salvation. You don’t pray three hours to prove to Him that you are more valuable than a Christian who prays thirty minutes.

You obey God because you belong to Him, because His grace calls you to a new life, and because authentic love produces a response.

Think about the difference between two motivations:

The motivation of fear: “I have to do enough things, or God will no longer accept me.”

The motivation of gratitude: “I have received God’s grace. I want my life to reflect what I say I believe.”

The first can feed guilt and exhaustion. The second places obedience in the context of the relationship with God.

This doesn’t mean obedience is optional or that grace justifies indifference. It means we must not confuse our response to grace with the price of grace.

You don’t work in order to become worthy of God’s love. You learn to live in line with the truth you have received through Christ.

3. A tree doesn’t produce fruit in order to become alive

In John 15:1–8, Jesus presents Himself as the vine and the disciples as the branches. He speaks about remaining in Him and about the fruit that results from this connection.

The image is important: fruit does not appear through a branch’s simple decision to try harder. It is tied to remaining in the vine.

Applied to the Christian life, this keeps us from two extremes.

The first extreme is passivity: “God will work anyway, so I don’t need to change anything.”

The second is activism without dependence on God: “If I do enough spiritual things, I will produce an authentic Christian life by myself.”

Jesus calls us neither to carelessness nor to self-sufficiency. He calls us to remain in Him.

Prayer, reading Scripture, fellowship, and obedience are not tricks by which we manufacture spiritual fruit. They are part of a life oriented toward God, in which we learn to hear His Word and respond to it.

Galatians 5:22–23 describes the fruit of the Spirit through traits such as love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control.

Notice the nature of this fruit. It is not a list of spectacular achievements. It doesn’t speak about the number of sermons listened to, the hours spent reading, or the number of people who know your name.

You can have an impressive spiritual discipline on the outside and still need growth in patience, gentleness, or self-control.

That is why, when you evaluate your progress, don’t ask only:

  • How many chapters have I read?
  • How many minutes have I prayed?
  • How many sermons have I listened to?

Ask also:

  • Am I becoming more attentive to how I treat others?
  • Am I learning to control my reactions?
  • Do I admit my mistakes more quickly?
  • Am I more faithful in my responsibilities?
  • Am I learning to tell the truth with love?
  • Am I using my gifts for the good of others?

You won’t answer all these questions perfectly. That is not the goal. The goal is to pursue real growth, not merely to accumulate the impression that you have grown.

4. Turn every study time into a step of obedience

A simple method can help you make the connection between Scripture and everyday life.

The “One truth → One response” method

1. Read

Read the passage attentively. Seek to understand what it says in context, not just what interesting idea you can extract from it.

2. Identify the truth

Formulate in a single sentence the main idea you understood from the passage.

3. Choose a concrete response

Decide what you can do differently, what you need to set right, or what responsibility you need to carry out.

4. Put it into practice

Take the first realistic step. Don’t wait until you feel you have become the perfect person before you begin to obey.

A practical example

Suppose you read Philippians 2:3–4, where Paul urges believers to give up selfish ambition and to consider the interests of others as well.

The truth identified: I must not pursue only my own advantage, but also consider the good of others.

The possible application: Today, I will listen attentively to a person without interrupting and without immediately turning the conversation into a story about me.

The action: When a coworker begins telling me about a problem, I put my phone aside and listen to him.

It seems like a small gesture. That is exactly where the challenge lies: not to despise ordinary listening just because it doesn’t seem spectacular.

Sometimes the most concrete response to a biblical truth is not to organize a big project, but to behave with love toward the person right in front of you.

5. Service begins before you receive a platform

Many Christians associate service with preaching, leading worship, public evangelism, or organizing events. These can be valuable forms of service, but they are not the whole Christian life.

In Mark 10:42–45, Jesus contrasts the way those who seek to exercise authority lead with the model the disciples are to follow. He presents Himself as the example of service.

In the Kingdom of God, importance is not measured only by visibility.

Service can look like this:

  • You help your classmate understand a difficult subject, without humiliating him.
  • You carry out your responsibilities seriously, even if no one praises you.
  • You help your family without waiting to be asked every time.
  • You use your time to listen to a person going through a hard period.
  • You admit your mistake and ask for forgiveness.
  • You use your skills to solve a real problem.
  • You get involved in the Christian community with humility, respecting guidance and healthy boundaries.

Colossians 3:23–24 urges believers to work heartily, as for the Lord. The context of the passage includes working relationships in the ancient world, marked by inequalities that we must not ignore. The principle of work done with integrity before God can also be applied to our responsibilities, without using the text to justify abuse or injustice.

So the spiritual life doesn’t begin after you have finished all your obligations and entered a religious space. Your faith also influences how you study, work, lead, collaborate, and treat your neighbor.

It doesn’t mean that every ordinary activity must be turned into a religious event. It means you can honor God through the character and integrity with which you carry out your responsibilities.

Don’t wait for a bigger platform in order to be faithful in the things you already have to do.

6. Don’t confuse service with agitation

There is also the opposite danger: after you understand that you must move to action, you begin to accept every opportunity, every responsibility, and every project.

You end up permanently busy, but not necessarily fruitful.

Being busy is not the same as being obedient. Sometimes constant activity can become a way to avoid quiet, the examination of your own motives, or responsibilities you don’t feel like carrying out.

In Luke 10:38–42, the episode of Martha and Mary invites us to examine our priorities in the presence of Jesus. The text must not be reduced to the idea that every practical work is wrong. The problem is how our concerns come to dominate us and distract us from what matters.

Before you accept a new responsibility, ask yourself:

  • Is it something good and fitting for me in this season?
  • What existing responsibility might I neglect if I accept?
  • Do I want to serve, or am I seeking the approval of people?
  • Can I carry out this task seriously?
  • Have I talked with the right people when the decision affects others too?

You don’t have to suspect every desire to help. But neither should you automatically call every form of overload a “spiritual sacrifice.”

God does not call you to prove that you are indispensable. The church, the family, and the community need faithful people, not people who wear themselves out trying to be everywhere.

Healthy service also includes the wisdom to say “no” when a responsibility would compromise other important duties.

7. How to turn truth into action over seven days

Don’t try to change every area of life in a single day. Choose a single biblical truth and seek to respond to it concretely.

The seven-day challenge

Don’t turn this challenge into a new test by which you measure your spiritual worth. If you miss a day, return to the next step. If you discover that you misinterpreted a text, correct your understanding. If a situation is more complex than you anticipated, seek wisdom and counsel.

The goal is not to check off seven boxes. The goal is to practice the connection between what you believe and how you live.

8. Questions for reflection

Set aside a few minutes to answer honestly, perhaps in a notebook.

  • What biblical truth do I already know but rarely apply?
  • In what area is it easier for me to talk about faith than to live it?
  • Is there a responsibility I am putting off while I consume Christian content?
  • What motivation pushes me to serve: love, gratitude, the approval of people, or the need to prove my worth?
  • Whom can I concretely help over the next seven days?
  • What small change could show that I take seriously the truth I have understood?

Don’t answer only with nice formulations. Look for concrete examples from your life. Sincerity before God is more useful than a carefully built spiritual image.

Lord Jesus Christ, thank You for the grace You have shown me and for the truth of Your Word.

Help me not to be content merely with listening and accumulating information. Give me wisdom to understand Scripture in its context and a heart willing to respond with obedience.

Show me where I need to change my attitude, where I need to correct my behavior, and where I need to take on my responsibilities with more faithfulness. Keep me from pride, from the desire to be seen, and from trying to earn my worth through religious activities.

Teach me to remain in You and to bear fruit through the work of the Holy Spirit. Help me to love people not only with words, but with fitting deeds, done with humility and discernment.

When I fail, teach me to return to You, to receive correction, and to keep growing. Let the time I spend in the Word and in prayer be reflected in my character, my choices, and my relationships.

Closing the chapter

You can end a day with many pages read and still avoid a step of obedience you already know. You can listen to a sermon about love and need to ask forgiveness. You can learn about faithfulness and be called to carry out an ordinary responsibility better.

These things don’t make prayer, Bible study, or preaching useless. On the contrary, they remind us why we need them.

Truth is not meant only to fill your mind, but to shape your life.

You don’t have to turn every verse into a complicated project. Begin by identifying the truth, understanding what it asks, and taking the next fitting step. Keep praying, studying, receiving guidance, and growing.

Chapter 12

A 30-Day Plan for a More Disciplined Life

You don’t need another promise made in a moment of enthusiasm. You need a way of life you can continue even when the enthusiasm fades.

You have reached the last chapter of the book. Up to here, you have examined how you use your attention, your relationship with your phone, procrastination, discipleship, prayer, the study of Scripture, rest, community, and service.

Now the practical question arises: how do you turn all these things into real life?

Maybe you have begun ambitious plans before. You decided you would get up earlier, read more of the Bible, pray daily, give up the time wasted on your phone, and organize your responsibilities better. The first days went well. Then tiredness appeared, your schedule changed, you lost a morning, and gradually you abandoned everything.

The problem is not necessarily that you lack good desires. Sometimes the problem is that you built a plan that worked only on ideal days.

A mature plan must take real life into account: responsibilities, sleep, busy periods, people who need you, and days when you don’t feel your best.

The 30 days in this chapter are not a deadline by which you will become a perfect Christian. Nor do they guarantee that all habits will be fixed permanently. They are a period of intentional practice, in which you will learn to organize your time, reduce distractions, and build a sustainable spiritual rhythm.

The goal is not to check off thirty flawless days.

The goal is that, after these thirty days, you will know better how to continue.

1. Establish the foundation before you build the schedule

Before you organize your mornings, you need to clarify why you are doing all this.

Spiritual discipline is not a method by which you make God accept you. Ephesians 2:8–10 places good works in the context of grace: salvation is God’s gift, and the believer’s life is called to reflect that work.

In John 15:4–5, Jesus speaks about remaining in Him. Bearing fruit is not reduced to individual effort, but depends on the relationship with Him.

So you don’t begin this plan by saying:

“Over the next thirty days I will prove that I am disciplined enough for God.”

You begin by saying:

“I want to use my time better, to grow in the knowledge of God, and to respond with obedience to the grace I have received.”

This difference influences your whole journey.

If on one day you don’t manage to follow the schedule, it doesn’t mean God loves you less. It means you need to look honestly at what happened and choose the next step.

If you have a very good day, it doesn’t mean you have become superior to other believers. It means you had a day in which certain things worked well.

Don’t turn discipline into a spiritual ranking.

2. The rules of the 30 days

For the next month, you will follow six principles.

1. Pursue consistency, not perfection.

Better a realistic rhythm you can continue than a spectacular schedule abandoned after four days.

2. Don’t let your phone decide your morning.

Begin the day intentionally. Don’t automatically open social media before you know what you have to do.

3. Read in order to understand and apply.

Don’t measure Bible study only by chapters covered. Stop at the text and seek to understand its message.

4. Put the important things on your schedule.

Don’t assume that prayer, study, and responsibilities will automatically get free time. Plan for them realistically.

5. Keep relationships and service in the equation.

The Christian life is not an individual optimization project. Love for God is also reflected in how you treat people.

6. Resume the plan after a missed day.

Don’t wait for the next Monday, the beginning of the month, or a new year. Return to the next possible step.

You won’t try to change everything at once. The month is divided into four weeks, each with a priority.

4. Week 1: Regain your attention

Days 1–7

In the first week you don’t need a revolution. You need to understand what your life really looks like.

In the first days, observe the time spent on your phone, the moments when you automatically go to social media, and the situations in which you use the screen to avoid a difficult task.

Don’t condemn yourself for what you discover. The data is useful precisely because it helps you make better decisions.

The week’s plan

The spiritual rhythm

If you don’t already have a steady rhythm, begin with a realistic version:

  • 10–15 minutes of prayer;
  • 10–15 minutes of attentive reading of Scripture;
  • 2–5 minutes to write down one truth and one practical response.

If you already have a more consistent rhythm, you don’t have to reduce your time artificially. Keep what works and focus on attention, understanding, and application.

At the end of the week, the main question is not “How productive was I?”, but “What did I find out about how I use my attention?”

5. Week 2: Build a spiritual rhythm

Days 8–14

In the second week, you will give more attention to prayer and Scripture.

Mark 1:35 shows us that Jesus withdrew for prayer. Luke 5:16 mentions that He withdrew to lonely places and prayed. These texts offer important examples about prayer, but they do not set a universal number of hours that every Christian must reach.

Your goal is to find a faithful and realistic rhythm in the context of your responsibilities.

The week’s plan

A simple model for Bible study

You can use four questions:

  • Context: Who is speaking, to whom, and in what situation?
  • Message: What does the passage communicate in its context?
  • Connection to Christ: How does the text fit into the broader message of Scripture and the work of Christ, where relevant?
  • Response: What should I believe, understand, correct, or do?

Don’t force every passage to deliver a spectacular revelation. Sometimes careful understanding of a familiar truth is more helpful than a constant search for an unusual interpretation.

6. Week 3: Move from discipline to obedience

Days 15–21

In this stage, you will seek to let the time spent in Scripture and prayer influence your decisions.

In James 1:22–25, the emphasis falls on doing the Word, not just hearing it. And in John 13:34–35, Jesus ties the identity of the disciples to the love they show one another.

So spiritual discipline must not make you merely more organized. It must be part of a life oriented toward God and toward the good of your neighbor.

The week’s plan

Don’t try to resolve all the conflicts in your life in a single week. Some situations require time, discernment, and healthy boundaries. The important thing is not to use spiritual study to avoid indefinitely a step of obedience you can take.

7. Week 4: Consolidate a long-term rhythm

Days 22–30

Now you are no longer seeking to keep adding new habits. You are seeking to keep what is valuable and eliminate what doesn’t work.

A routine is not good just because it looks good on paper. It is good if it helps you carry out your responsibilities, keep your relationship with God at the center of your life, and live with more attention and integrity.

The week’s plan

On the last day, don’t ask only whether you managed to keep to the schedule. Ask what you have learned about yourself, what you need to continue, and what you need to change.

8. Build your personal schedule

A good plan must fit your life, not an imaginary life in which delays, tiredness, or unforeseen obligations never appear.

Fill out the plan below for the next 30 days. It is a starting point, not a contract that obliges you to function like a robot with Bible verses stuck on its casing.

My plan for 30 days

Choose goals that you can adapt on busy days.

Important: adjust the schedule if the hours you chose don’t allow you to sleep enough or to carry out your obligations. For most adults, the general recommendation is at least seven hours of sleep a night, and teenagers usually need more. Discipline does not mean sacrificing the rest you need in order to gather hours of spiritual activity.

If you want to gradually reach two or three hours of prayer and Bible study a day, treat this as a personal goal, not as a universal standard of Christian maturity. Build it progressively and only to the extent that it fits your responsibilities.

9. How to measure your progress without falling into legalism

At the end of each day, give yourself two minutes to reflect.

You don’t have to note every detail, nor turn your relationship with God into a performance table. Use the evaluation as a tool for understanding and adjustment.

End-of-day evaluation

Check off what really happened. Not what you wished had happened.

Don’t interpret a difficult day as a verdict on your spiritual life. A table like this cannot measure God’s love for you, your worth, or the whole work of the Holy Spirit in your life.

It can only help you notice behaviors, identify obstacles, and make wiser decisions.

At the weekly evaluation, look for patterns:

  • If you constantly miss morning prayer, maybe the interval you chose is unrealistic.
  • If you lose a lot of time on your phone when you are tired, maybe you need rest and clearer rules for its use.
  • If you read a lot but don’t retain the main ideas, maybe you need to slow down.
  • If you get involved in many activities but neglect your responsibilities, maybe you need to reduce the number of commitments.
  • If you constantly feel guilty that you are not doing enough, examine whether you have built unrealistic goals or whether you measure your worth by performance.

A good plan doesn’t just ask you to try harder. It helps you understand what needs to change.

10. What do you do after the 30 days?

This is where it shows whether you have understood the purpose of the whole process.

You don’t have to automatically repeat the identical schedule. You don’t have to immediately add another hour of prayer, another biblical chapter, or five more goals.

Instead, answer the following questions:

What do I want to keep?

Identify two or three habits that have been good for you and that you can realistically continue.

What do I need to modify?

Acknowledge what was too ambitious, what didn’t fit your schedule, and what obstacles remain.

What do I need to go deeper into?

Maybe you need to understand a book of the Bible better, grow in your prayer life, seek spiritual guidance, or work on a particular attitude.

How will I continue to live in community?

Don’t build a system in which your whole spiritual life depends on a personal schedule. Keep fellowship with other believers, participation in the life of the church, and relationships in which you can receive guidance and correction.

What is the next concrete step?

Choose one main goal for the next month. Write it down clearly and decide when you will begin.

You don’t have to reorganize your whole life every week. You need a rhythm you can maintain and a willingness to correct it when necessary.

11. The last challenge: don’t let the book remain just a book

You can finish this book and feel that you have understood many things. You may even agree with every chapter.

But if you close the book and keep living exactly the same way, the information has not reached its practical purpose.

You don’t have to change everything today. Choose a single thing you have understood and put it into practice.

Maybe you need to protect your morning from digital distractions.

Maybe you need to set a consistent interval for Scripture.

Maybe you need to stop putting off a responsibility.

Maybe you need to ask someone’s forgiveness.

Maybe you need to learn to rest without guilt.

Maybe you need to give up the idea that a good spiritual day is a day in which you accomplished everything you set out to do.

Don’t necessarily look for a spectacular change. Look for the next step of obedience.

In Luke 16:10, Jesus speaks about faithfulness in little things. The principle reminds us that faithfulness doesn’t begin only in big moments, when everyone is watching. It is also seen in ordinary choices, repeated over time.

Your discipline must not become your main identity. You are called to follow Christ, not to become an admirer of your own schedule.

Use the tools, but don’t worship them. Organize your time, but don’t forget for Whom and for what you use it. Study Scripture, but let its truth correct your own conclusions too. Pray with perseverance, but don’t turn prayer into a contest. Serve, but don’t confuse visibility with fruitfulness.

And when you fall back into old habits, don’t turn the fall into a reason to give up. Acknowledge what happened, return to God, and resume the next step.

A word to the reader

If you have come this far, don’t consider that you have finished a process of self-improvement. You have worked through an invitation to examination, understanding, and practice.

Not all your problems will disappear in thirty days. Some habits take longer. Some struggles need the support of other people. Some periods will be busier than others.

You don’t have to live every day at the same intensity in order to remain faithful. You need discernment, perseverance, and a willingness to return to what matters.

The ultimate goal is not the number of pages read, hours accumulated, or boxes checked. The goal is to follow Christ with a sincere heart and a life that increasingly reflects His truth.

Use your time wisely. Guard your attention. Grow in the knowledge of Scripture. Pray. Love people. Carry out your responsibilities. Rest when you need to. And don’t forget that discipline is a tool, not a god.

In a world that constantly tries to capture your attention, the choice to live consciously and to follow Jesus is one worth making every day.

“Redeeming the time, because the days are evil.” — Ephesians 5:16

Not to fill every minute with activity, but to live wisely the time you have been given.
